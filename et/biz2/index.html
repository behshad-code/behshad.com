Business 2.0
Leading change across the enterprise.

Growth and acquisitions had made a multibillion-dollar networking and broadband business increasingly complex to run. Through Business 2.0, I led the business systems and applications contribution to an enterprise-wide transformation, connecting business priorities, architecture, and delivery while keeping day-to-day operations in view.

How the business case was built

Finance and functional teams developed the estimate using the 2017 budget, discovery workshops, procurement spend analysis, and operating assumptions. Run-rate represents the estimated recurring annual benefit once the improvements are in place.

The $40–57M headline is the steering committee's reported forecast. Supporting category estimates use different rounding and do not reconcile exactly to that headline. The estimate combines operating savings, productivity opportunities, and inventory cost-of-capital benefits.

Every design choice carried
a business consequence.

Leading the systems and applications work meant being accountable for how the pieces fit together. Business ambition, technical dependencies, and organizational readiness had to meet in a workable delivery plan.

01

Standardization versus business differentiation

The program favored common processes and standard functionality. Existing capabilities still needed to be evaluated for the value they provided.

What was at stake?
Business consequenceToo much variation increases integration and support demands. Removing a useful capability can disrupt how a team serves the business.
Leadership responsibilityConnect business justification with architecture impact so exceptions are deliberate and owned.
02

Speed to value versus transition complexity

Finance and supply chain sequencing affected how soon capabilities could be introduced and how much temporary integration the organization would carry.

What was at stake?
Business consequenceAn earlier release may unlock value sooner, but add interim connections and rework. A larger combined release concentrates operational and adoption risk.
Leadership responsibilityKeep dependencies, business readiness, and continuity visible alongside the delivery schedule.
03

Implementation capacity versus lasting ownership

The delivery model called for dedicated business participation, knowledge transfer, and a growing role for internal teams.

What was at stake?
Business consequenceA new platform creates ongoing responsibilities for process decisions, support, controls, and improvement.
Leadership responsibilityAccount for the people and capabilities needed to operate the solution as part of the transformation itself.
My accountability connected the business objective to the architecture, the delivery dependencies, and the teams expected to make it work.
