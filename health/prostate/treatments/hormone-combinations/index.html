Hormone therapy and combination treatments

Why androgen suppression is used in certain prostate cancers and what 2026 high-risk trials actually studied.

Editorial draft v2.0 · Evidence checked November 6, 2026 · Not clinically peer reviewed

Prostate cancer often relies on androgen signaling. Medicines that reduce testosterone or block androgen pathways can be important, but their benefits and adverse effects depend on disease stage and the treatments combined with them.

Key distinction: Some 2026 high-risk regimens remain evolving; regulatory and guideline status must be rechecked before publication.

ADT and androgen receptor agents

Androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) reduces testosterone activity through medication or surgery. Androgen receptor pathway inhibitors block or interfere with androgen signaling in other ways. These treatments are not interchangeable with local treatment for every patient.

In selected localized disease, ADT can be combined with radiation; in metastatic disease it is often part of systemic treatment. Common consequences include hot flashes, sexual symptoms, fatigue, bone loss, metabolic changes and possible cardiovascular concerns.

PROTEUS: before and after high-risk surgery

The phase 3 PROTEUS trial randomized 2,109 men with high-risk localized or locally advanced cancer planned for prostatectomy. Both groups received ADT; one also received apalutamide and the other placebo before and after surgery. Five-year metastasis-free survival was 78.2% versus 73.5%, respectively.

Grade 3 or 4 adverse events were reported in 39.6% of the apalutamide arm versus 31.0% in the control arm. This is an important peer-reviewed result, but it applies to a highly selected population and is not a general recommendation for low-risk patients.

Supporting sources: New England Journal of Medicine (2026), opens source in new tab

Interpreting newer trial reports

Trials of additional drugs with radiation, such as DASL-HiCaP, have generated promising reports in very high-risk patients. Their treatment combinations, study populations and follow-up maturity differ from PROTEUS. A conference headline does not establish overall-survival benefit or regulatory approval.

A patient-facing summary should say whether results are interim, which endpoint improved, and whether harms or other options were measured. Before treatment selection, confirm current specialist guidance and the drug's approval for the intended setting.

Supporting sources: AUA/ASTRO (2026), opens source in new tab · European Association of Urology (2026), opens source in new tab

Managing long-term health on ADT

Loss of muscle, fatigue, bone-density changes, metabolic risk and sexual effects deserve proactive discussion. A care plan may involve appropriate exercise, cardiovascular risk management, bone-health evaluation and symptom support, adjusted for overall health and disease burden.

Ask who coordinates follow-up among oncology, primary care and supportive services. Never start or stop hormone medicines based on a research summary alone.

Supporting sources: National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab · European Association of Urology (2026), opens source in new tab

Questions to bring to your appointment

  • Why is hormone therapy appropriate for my disease state?
  • What benefit does the combination offer compared with its exact control group?
  • How are bone, heart, metabolic and sexual side effects monitored?
  • Is the proposed drug approved for this specific treatment setting?
