What happens during robotic prostatectomy?

A patient-centered explanation of the major stages, the reasons for each step, and what follows surgery.

Editorial draft v2.0 · Evidence checked November 6, 2026 · Not clinically peer reviewed

Robot-assisted radical prostatectomy is major cancer surgery performed through small access points. The surgeon controls instruments and makes the operative decisions; the robot does not independently remove the cancer.

Key distinction: A general conceptual guide, not surgical instruction; sequence and approach vary.

Before the operation

The treatment team reviews PSA, MRI, biopsy pathology, staging information, previous operations, medications and health risks. They discuss whether nerve sparing is oncologically safe and whether pelvic lymph-node dissection is indicated. The choice of robotic versus other surgery is only one part of informed consent.

Under general anesthesia, the surgical team positions the patient and creates access to the pelvis. Standard multiport surgery uses several small incisions. Certain single-port approaches use one access site but different internal routes.

Supporting sources: National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab · AUA/ASTRO (2026), opens source in new tab

Removing the gland while protecting nearby structures

The surgeon separates tissues around the bladder neck, prostate, seminal vesicles and rectum. Nerves supporting erections lie near the gland; preservation depends partly on where cancer is suspected and how safely a surgeon can dissect. A cancer-free surgical margin remains a primary objective.

The prostate and seminal vesicles are removed. When clinically indicated, lymph nodes may also be sampled. The bladder is reconnected to the remaining urethra and a temporary urinary catheter is left to allow healing.

Supporting sources: Lancet Oncology (2018), opens source in new tab · Lancet Oncology (2025), opens source in new tab

What the robot changes

Magnified three-dimensional vision and wristed instruments can help access the deep pelvis. Minimally invasive surgery may involve less blood loss and shorter hospitalization than traditional open surgery. These differences should not be translated into a guarantee of superior long-term continence, erections or cancer control.

In a Brisbane randomized trial, urinary and sexual outcomes at 24 months were similar between robotic and open surgery. Technologies have evolved since the trial, but choosing a team only for a machine model remains a poor substitute for evaluating experience and outcomes.

Supporting sources: Lancet Oncology (2018), opens source in new tab

Recovery and the pathology conversation

Catheter duration and discharge timing depend on the approach, complications and local protocols. Leakage after catheter removal is common and erectile recovery can be slower. Final pathology may revise the assessment of grade or extent.

Ask when catheter removal, pathology review and the first PSA check will occur; who handles urgent symptoms; and how pelvic-floor and sexual rehabilitation will be coordinated. Individual discharge instructions take precedence over generic timelines.

Supporting sources: National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab · American Urological Association (2024), opens source in new tab

Questions to bring to your appointment

  • Who performs the critical stages of the operation?
  • Will nerve sparing be attempted on one or both sides?
  • Will lymph nodes be removed, and why?
  • How will pathology, rehabilitation and PSA follow-up be organized?
