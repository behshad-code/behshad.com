Focal therapy: promising concept, important uncertainties

What HIFU, irreversible electroporation and other targeted approaches can and cannot establish.

Updated November 6, 2026 · Men's health education · Not clinically peer reviewed

Focal therapy aims to treat a cancerous region while leaving more prostate tissue in place. The appeal is understandable, but less treatment does not automatically mean equivalent cancer control or freedom from side effects.

Key distinction: Avoid absolute cure or potency claims; long-term comparative evidence remains limited.

What focal treatment attempts

Methods can include high-intensity focused ultrasound, cryotherapy or irreversible electroporation (IRE, sometimes marketed as NanoKnife). Imaging and biopsy are used to identify a target, but prostate cancer may be multifocal and areas outside the target may contain clinically important disease.

Preserving tissue could help some men avoid certain harms of whole-gland treatment, yet treatment location, nerve proximity, baseline function and cancer volume still affect urinary and sexual outcomes.

Evidence is different from established surgery and radiation

Many focal-therapy reports are small, nonrandomized or have shorter follow-up than studies of established whole-gland treatments. Comparisons can be complicated because patients chosen for focal treatment may have different risks from patients assigned to surgery or radiation.

NICE's 2026 IRE guidance recommends special arrangements for clinical governance, consent, and audit or research. This is not an endorsement of marketing claims that a one-hour procedure cures prostate cancer or eliminates the need for follow-up.

Supporting sources: NICE (2026), opens source in new tab · AUA/ASTRO (2026), opens source in new tab

Monitoring still matters

Cancer may persist in the treated region or later be detected elsewhere in the prostate. PSA interpretation is more complex when prostate tissue remains, and treatment teams may recommend serial MRI, PSA testing and additional biopsy.

Before proceeding, ask what constitutes failure, which follow-up procedures are required, and how salvage surgery or radiation would be approached if the cancer returns.

Supporting sources: European Association of Urology (2026), opens source in new tab · National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab

What to ask about a clinical trial

A clinical trial or specialized multidisciplinary evaluation can clarify eligibility and what is known about the proposed device. Ask for independently published cancer-control outcomes, duration of follow-up, adverse effects, competing alternatives, and whether all outcomes were collected systematically.

Regulatory availability of a device does not prove that a particular cancer treatment protocol has superior long-term outcomes. Separate the promise of precision from the evidence for durable benefit.

Supporting sources: NICE (2026), opens source in new tab · AUA/ASTRO (2026), opens source in new tab

What must be shown before a focal procedure becomes routine care

Treating only a mapped portion of the gland may reduce injury to uninvolved tissue, but untreated prostate tissue remains and can contain additional disease. Success cannot be defined merely as an uncomplicated procedure, same-day discharge or a short-term PSA drop. It requires durable cancer control, clearly defined recurrence, biopsy and MRI follow-up, retreatment rates and meaningful urinary and sexual outcomes.

Studies of HIFU, irreversible electroporation and other ablation techniques vary substantially in disease selection and follow-up. NICE's position on irreversible electroporation includes special governance and audit or research arrangements, not an unrestricted cure claim. If offered a focal procedure, ask whether a randomized comparison exists, how recurrence will be detected and what happens if whole-gland treatment is later required.

Supporting sources: NICE (2026), opens source in new tab · European Association of Urology (2026), opens source in new tab · AUA/ASTRO (2026), opens source in new tab · National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab

Questions to bring to your appointment

  • Is this procedure considered standard care, special governance, or research?
  • How might cancer outside the treated region be found?
  • Which long-term comparative outcomes are published?
  • What follow-up biopsies and salvage options would be required?
