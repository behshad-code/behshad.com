Radiation options for localized prostate cancer

From conventional schedules to five-session SBRT and brachytherapy, with benefits and late side effects.

Editorial draft v2.0 · Evidence checked November 6, 2026 · Not clinically peer reviewed

Radiation is not a single technique. The number of sessions, targeting method, use of internal radiation and whether hormone therapy is combined all depend on disease risk and anatomy.

Key distinction: PACE-B's results are applicable to its defined low/intermediate-risk population, not all high-risk disease.

External beam: several schedules

Standard external-beam radiotherapy may be given over multiple weeks. Moderate hypofractionation delivers somewhat larger doses per session over fewer visits, while stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) uses very precise, high-dose treatment over about five sessions in eligible patients.

Fewer appointments can reduce travel and time away from work, but shorter courses are not automatically better for every person's urinary function, gland size or cancer characteristics.

Supporting sources: AUA/ASTRO (2026), opens source in new tab · National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab

The PACE-B comparison

PACE-B randomized 874 patients with low- or intermediate-risk localized prostate cancer to five-session SBRT or longer conventional/moderately hypofractionated radiation. At five years, freedom from biochemical or clinical failure was 95.8% with SBRT and 94.6% with the longer schedule, meeting the study's noninferiority criterion.

Moderate-or-worse late urinary toxicity was higher with SBRT, 26.9% versus 18.3%, while reported bowel toxicity was similar. These are group results, not individual predictions. Importantly, PACE-B did not compare radiation with surgery.

Supporting sources: New England Journal of Medicine (2024), opens source in new tab

Brachytherapy and combined treatments

Brachytherapy places radioactive sources in or near the prostate. It may be an option alone for certain risk categories or combined with external radiation for others. Suitability depends on urinary symptoms, anatomy, cancer risk and access to an experienced specialist.

For some unfavorable intermediate-risk or high-risk cases, androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) is added to radiation because randomized evidence supports combined treatment in specified populations. Duration and side effects require an individual discussion.

Supporting sources: AUA/ASTRO (2026), opens source in new tab · National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab

Plan for long-term effects

After radiation, PSA typically declines gradually to a nadir rather than becoming undetectable immediately. Temporary PSA bounces can occur. New urinary or rectal bleeding after treatment should be assessed and not automatically assumed to be radiation injury.

Ask the treatment team about bladder and bowel preparation, baseline urinary symptoms, supportive care and how long-term outcomes are tracked. A multidisciplinary comparison can be valuable before choosing a schedule.

Supporting sources: National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab · European Association of Urology (2026), opens source in new tab

Questions to bring to your appointment

  • Which radiation schedules are evidence-supported for my risk group?
  • What is the absolute chance of significant urinary or bowel side effects?
  • Would hormone therapy be recommended and for how long?
  • How will PSA follow-up and late symptoms be handled?
