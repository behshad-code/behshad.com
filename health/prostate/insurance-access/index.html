When insurance delays a test: practical steps to challenge a denial

The clinical discussion and the coverage discussion are not always the same. A test can be recommended by a clinician but still require prior authorization or additional documentation. A denial is a decision to understand and address, not necessarily the end of the process.

Start by getting the exact reason

Ask the insurer and the ordering office whether the issue is lack of prior authorization, missing clinical documentation, network status, coding, an exclusion or a determination about medical necessity. Request the written denial and the plan's appeal instructions. Coverage rules and deadlines differ by policy and insurance program.

If a test is being delayed, ask the clinician how urgent it is and whether another clinically appropriate diagnostic path can proceed while coverage is reviewed. Do not simply allow a recommended follow-up date to disappear from the calendar.

What a prostate-specific denial should identify

For MRI, biopsy, a biomarker or specialized imaging, ask for the exact test name, ordering clinician's clinical question, relevant PSA or pathology history, the denial reason and any cited policy. Missing documentation, network issues, coding or medical-necessity findings may require different next steps.

Work with the ordering clinician

  1. Identify the precise test or service. Get its name, ordering physician, diagnosis context and any referral or authorization number.
  2. Ask what clinical facts were included. The office may need to provide the relevant history, prior testing, risk factors, imaging or medical-necessity rationale.
  3. Request a peer-to-peer review when appropriate. Some plans allow the treating clinician to speak with the reviewing clinician. The ordering office can explain what is available.
  4. Track deadlines. Save the denial date, appeal deadline, contact number, case reference and the person responsible for the next action.
  5. Ask about expedited review if medically urgent. Eligibility depends on the circumstances and plan rules; the clinician can help document why waiting may be harmful.

Advanced testing may need different evidence

For genomic testing, PSMA PET or newer systemic treatments, an insurer may ask about stage, pathology, prior treatment and the test's intended role. The clinician can clarify which current guideline, approved indication or supporting evidence applies. FDA authorization does not automatically guarantee coverage.

Internal appeal and external review

Many health plans provide an internal appeal process and, for eligible disputes, an external review by an independent party. HealthCare.gov and CMS describe these rights, including when external review may be available. Medicare and Medicare Advantage have their own pathways and deadlines, so do not assume one set of rules applies to every insurance plan.

Use the insurer's actual denial letter and benefit documents to determine the next step. Patient advocates or state consumer assistance programs may also help explain the process. If an appeal is submitted, obtain confirmation it was received and a date to follow up.

Appeal pathways depend on insurance type

Commercial employer plans, Marketplace coverage, Original Medicare, Medicare Advantage and Medicaid may follow different appeal procedures and deadlines. Follow the written denial notice and official program guidance. If waiting may cause clinical harm, ask the treating clinician whether an expedited review is appropriate.

Keep the clinical plan visible during an appeal

Track the authorization and appeal numbers, notice dates, records submitted, required next response and the clinician responsible for follow-up. Keep clinical appointments and recommended monitoring on the calendar when possible. This information belongs in the patient's own records; the website does not collect it. Questions that keep the process moving

Ask the ordering office and insurer

  • What specific evidence or policy criterion is missing?
  • Has the ordering clinician received the full denial?
  • Is there an opportunity to resubmit, seek peer review or file an appeal?
  • Can the clinician document why this test is necessary in my circumstances?
  • What is the appeal deadline, and who is tracking it?
  • Is expedited review appropriate, and what happens clinically while we wait?
  • If this is not covered, is there a clinically reasonable alternative?

Insurance coverage decisions do not determine what is medically appropriate. Equally, a requested test is not automatically necessary for every patient. The most effective challenge connects the specific clinical question to documentation and the plan's review rules.

Rehabilitation is also an access question

Coverage problems may affect pelvic-floor therapy, sexual rehabilitation, counseling or specialist visits as well as tests and drugs. Ask which service or referral is missing, whether a covered alternative is clinically reasonable and what documentation supports the need. Do not assume one insurer's policy applies to all plans.
