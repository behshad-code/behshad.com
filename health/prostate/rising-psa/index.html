Screening & PSA

A rising PSA: what it may mean and what to ask next

An elevated PSA is not a cancer diagnosis. It is also not a result to dismiss without understanding why it changed. The useful next step is to turn an uncertain number into a clear, individualized follow-up plan.

Start with the distinction that matters

Prostate-specific antigen (PSA) is made by both normal and cancerous prostate cells. Benign enlargement (BPH), inflammation, infection, recent procedures and other factors can raise it. Many prostate cancers cause no urinary symptoms in their early stages, so feeling well does not settle the question either way.

There is no single PSA cutoff that definitively separates cancer from a benign condition. Age, family history, inherited risks, previous results, medicines, prostate examination and health history help determine what a result means.

Keep both truths in view: An elevated PSA does not automatically mean cancer. A normal-feeling urinary system does not automatically rule it out.

A symptom is not a screening test

Early localized prostate cancer often causes no symptoms. Weak stream, urgency and nighttime urination are common with benign enlargement or other urinary conditions, but new symptoms still deserve evaluation. A symptom assessment and a discussion about screening are related, not identical.

What to do after an unexpected result

  1. Confirm the result. Ask whether and when to repeat PSA before moving to more invasive tests. Temporary elevations are common.
  2. Check circumstances that affect the test. Discuss possible infection, prostatitis, recent prostate procedures, vigorous cycling, ejaculation and drugs such as finasteride or dutasteride, which can lower PSA readings.
  3. Look at prior results, not only the latest one. Bring previous PSA values, dates and lab names. A change can motivate review, but the rate of change alone should not be the sole reason for biopsy.
  4. Clarify individual risk. Age, family history, inherited mutations, and other factors can shift the balance between observing, testing further and biopsy.
  5. Agree on a specific next action and date. “We'll keep an eye on it” is not a complete plan unless everyone knows what will be repeated, when, and what would trigger the next step.

The National Cancer Institute notes that clinicians may repeat an abnormal screening PSA after several weeks before proceeding. The timing and tests depend on the clinical situation.

Agree on a result owner and next test

When PSA remains elevated, confirm who will review the next result, what other information might change the plan and when follow-up should occur. Repeat testing, imaging or biopsy timing depends on individual risk; the fact that PSA has changed is not enough to identify the cause.

PSA density is calculated as PSA divided by prostate volume. It can be useful when a larger gland may partly explain a higher PSA, but the value needs to be considered with MRI and other findings. A clinician may also use a validated risk calculator.

A stepwise path, not a single cutoff

After a persistent elevation, clinicians may consider PSA density, a calibrated risk calculator, MRI or selected biomarkers if the result would meaningfully change whether biopsy is recommended. IsoPSA and Stockholm3 serve narrower risk-estimation purposes and are not interchangeable screening or diagnostic tests. A reassuring MRI or marker cannot exclude every important cancer. Screening is a decision, not a slogan

Screening may detect potentially harmful cancer earlier, but it can also lead to false alarms, biopsy complications and treatment of cancers that might never have caused problems. This is why professional organizations emphasize informed discussions and consideration of individual risk rather than a universal rule for every man.

The American Cancer Society recommends starting the screening conversation earlier for men at higher risk. Whether screening is appropriate and how often to repeat it depends on age, health, risk and preferences. Once a result is abnormal, discussing it with a clinician is different from making an initial screening decision.

Guidelines differ across countries

The 2026 AUA/SUO, EAU and Australian PCFA guidance favor informed, risk-adapted decisions, but they differ in screening age and interval details. The final Australian guidance is not the same as an earlier age-40 draft proposal. Compare the guidelines by region →

A practical appointment checklist

Questions worth taking to the appointment

  • What are the most plausible explanations for this result?
  • Should the PSA test be repeated? When, and under what preparation?
  • How does this compare with prior results, my age and risk factors?
  • Would knowing prostate volume or PSA density change the assessment?
  • Would MRI or another biomarker meaningfully change what we do next?
  • What would lead you to recommend a biopsy, and what could be missed by waiting?
  • If we monitor, what is the test schedule and what is the trigger to reassess?

Keep a simple record of dates, results, laboratory, medications and next appointments. The purpose is to support a conversation, not to diagnose yourself from a spreadsheet.

Take away a clear follow-up agreement

Before leaving the appointment, confirm the next test, an approximate date, who will explain the result and which change would trigger further imaging or biopsy. Keep the details in your own records rather than entering medical information on a public website. Build a printable question list →
