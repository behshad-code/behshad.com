Beyond the PSA number

What PSA density, free PSA, risk calculators and additional blood tests can add to a screening discussion.

Updated November 6, 2026 · Men's health education · Not clinically peer reviewed

PSA measures a protein, not a cancer. Doctors may combine it with other information to decide whether testing should stop, be repeated, or progress to MRI or biopsy.

Key distinction: Different biomarkers and regulatory indications are not directly interchangeable.

The value of context and repeat measurement

PSA can rise with benign enlargement, inflammation and procedures, and values can fluctuate between tests. Finasteride and dutasteride can lower measured PSA. Clinicians interpret values using prior results, medications and the clinical situation rather than treating one reading as a definitive diagnosis.

PSA velocity, or change over time, may support a discussion but should not by itself drive a biopsy decision. An unexpected elevation may reasonably be confirmed first unless there are other indications for prompt evaluation.

PSA density and free PSA

PSA density divides the PSA result by prostate volume, often estimated by imaging. It can help distinguish a PSA that might partly reflect a large prostate from a result that merits further evaluation. Percent-free PSA compares the unbound portion of PSA with total PSA in contexts where that information may improve risk estimation.

Neither measure establishes whether cancer exists. Cutoffs vary with patient selection and local clinical protocols, and a lower-risk estimate can still miss clinically significant disease.

Supporting sources: European Association of Urology (2026), opens source in new tab · AUA/SUO (2026), opens source in new tab

Specialized tests answer narrower questions

Tests such as PHI, 4Kscore, IsoPSA and Stockholm3 combine biochemical markers or clinical factors to estimate the chance that biopsy will find important cancer. Their populations, availability, thresholds, regulatory status and evidence differ. IsoPSA's U.S. authorization is for a defined group already being considered for biopsy, not universal screening.

A 2026 Swedish follow-up found Stockholm3 promising for detecting clinically significant cancers, but it does not establish improved cancer-specific survival or prove that all health systems should adopt it. Newer experimental blood and urine tests require clinical validation before routine use.

Supporting sources: AUA/SUO (2026), opens source in new tab · Annals of Internal Medicine (2026), opens source in new tab · U.S. FDA (2025), opens source in new tab

Choosing a next step

A risk calculator or biomarker can help when the result would genuinely change whether a biopsy is recommended. MRI can locate suspicious areas and help target sampling, while biopsy remains the usual method of confirming cancer and its grade.

Ask what a specific test will change, not merely whether it exists. A strong plan explains what happens after high, intermediate and reassuring results.

Supporting sources: European Association of Urology (2026), opens source in new tab · JAMA Oncology (2024), opens source in new tab

Comparing a second test with the decision it is meant to improve

A useful biomarker does not need to diagnose every prostate cancer. Some are designed to estimate the probability of Grade Group 2 or higher cancer among men already considering biopsy. Their utility depends on sensitivity, specificity, whether a false negative would delay treatment and whether a negative result changes the decision enough to avoid an invasive procedure.

Stockholm3 and IsoPSA have different evidence bases and intended uses; they are not interchangeable, and a study in one country's screening population may not generalize to another clinical setting. A patient should ask what validation population resembles them, whether the test is authorized and covered locally, and which follow-up is recommended if the result is low-risk but PSA or MRI remains concerning.

Supporting sources: Annals of Internal Medicine (2026), opens source in new tab · U.S. FDA (2025), opens source in new tab · AUA/SUO (2026), opens source in new tab

Questions to bring to your appointment

  • Would repeating PSA change the decision?
  • Would prostate volume or PSA density clarify risk?
  • What additional test has been validated for my situation?
  • If a marker is reassuring, what risk still remains?
