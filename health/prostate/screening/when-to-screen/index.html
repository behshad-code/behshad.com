When should PSA screening start?

How U.S., European and Australian recommendations differ, and why risk matters more than one birthday.

Updated November 6, 2026 · Men's health education · Not clinically peer reviewed

There is no universal age at which every man must start or stop PSA screening. Professional guidance weighs possible reduction in prostate cancer deaths against false positives, overdiagnosis and treatment harms.

Key distinction: US, EU and Australian organizations do not publish one interchangeable screening schedule.

The U.S. conversation

The American Urological Association's early detection guideline emphasizes shared decision-making and risk-adapted PSA testing. The American Cancer Society encourages an informed conversation with a clinician, with earlier discussions for those at higher risk. Exact timing depends on health, life expectancy, family history, inherited variants and preferences.

A PSA screening result is not a diagnosis. An unexpected elevation often warrants repeat testing and individualized assessment before an imaging or biopsy decision. Testing every man at the same interval can miss meaningful differences in baseline risk.

Europe uses explicit risk groups

The EAU's 2026 guidance discusses earlier PSA testing for men at higher risk, including those with family history, African ancestry or BRCA2 mutations. It also considers life expectancy and baseline PSA in designing follow-up intervals. This is guidance for informed patients and their clinicians, not a reason to import a single European threshold into every setting.

Risk calculators and MRI can help interpret a persistent abnormal result. Screening intervals should not be confused with follow-up schedules for a man who already has elevated PSA or a prior biopsy.

Supporting sources: European Association of Urology (2026), opens source in new tab · European Association of Urology (2026), opens source in new tab

Australia updated its guideline in 2026

Australia's final PCFA early-detection recommendations, approved by NHMRC on May 18, 2026, emphasize clinician-initiated discussion and risk-based testing. Average-risk recommendations focus on ages 50 to 69; conversations start earlier for specified higher-risk groups. The earlier draft proposal for a routine age-40 baseline is not the final recommendation.

Readers outside Australia should treat those ages as jurisdiction-specific context. A particular person's risk, health and choice matter more than importing rules across healthcare systems.

Supporting sources: Prostate Cancer Foundation of Australia (2026), opens source in new tab · NHMRC (2026), opens source in new tab

What screening can and cannot accomplish

PSA-based screening can help find cancers that may benefit from early treatment, but it can also lead to unnecessary investigations or treatment of slow-growing disease. Modern diagnostic approaches aim to reduce avoidable biopsies, yet a reassuring MRI or marker does not eliminate all risk.

A useful screening discussion ends with a plan: whether to test, what result would trigger reassessment, when the test will be repeated and who will review the result.

Supporting sources: National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab · AUA/SUO (2026), opens source in new tab

How to use guidelines without treating them as universal rules

The 2026 AUA/SUO and Australian PCFA/NHMRC recommendations both use risk and shared decision-making, but their proposed starting ages, intervals and referral thresholds reflect different health systems. A patient reading an online age recommendation should check whether it applies to average risk, higher inherited risk or an existing elevated PSA. Earlier Australian draft proposals should not be confused with the approved 2026 version.

Screening is valuable when the chance of avoiding a harmful cancer outweighs overdiagnosis and the downstream effects of further tests. Ask the clinician to explain what screening aims to prevent, what a positive result would lead to and when screening may no longer offer meaningful benefit given health status and life expectancy.

Supporting sources: AUA/SUO (2026), opens source in new tab · American Cancer Society (2026), opens source in new tab · National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab · Prostate Cancer Foundation of Australia (2026), opens source in new tab

Questions to bring to your appointment

  • How does my family history or ancestry affect the discussion?
  • What are the possible benefits and harms of PSA testing for me?
  • If we test, what would change the follow-up interval?
  • How would an elevated result be confirmed before biopsy?
