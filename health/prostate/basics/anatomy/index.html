Prostate anatomy and function

Where the prostate sits, what it does, and why neighboring structures matter during treatment.

Updated November 6, 2026 · Men's health education · Not clinically peer reviewed

The prostate is a small gland with a large effect on medical decisions. Its position explains urinary symptoms, and its close relationship to the urinary sphincter and erectile nerves helps explain the tradeoffs of surgery.

Key distinction: Anatomical explanations are general. Patient anatomy and surgical plans vary.

A small gland in a crowded space

The prostate sits below the bladder and surrounds the first part of the urethra, the channel that carries urine out of the body. It contributes fluid to semen. The seminal vesicles behind the bladder contribute additional fluid; their ducts connect with the prostate. The rectum lies directly behind the gland, which is why prostate examination and some treatments involve the rectal region.

The prostate commonly enlarges with age. Enlargement can compress the urethra and interfere with urine flow without being cancer. The organ's shape and position also explain why a procedure intended to relieve obstruction is different from an operation to remove the entire prostate.

The structures that matter during surgery

Near the prostate's outer sides run networks of nerves and vessels that contribute to erections. Below it, the external urinary sphincter helps maintain continence. The bladder neck connects the bladder to the prostate and urethra; supporting tissues and ligaments help stabilize this area.

During radical prostatectomy, the gland and seminal vesicles are removed and the bladder is reconnected to the remaining urethra. Cancer location may limit how close the surgeon can dissect while preserving nerves. No technique can guarantee both cancer clearance and perfect recovery of sexual or urinary function.

Supporting sources: Lancet Oncology (2018), opens source in new tab · Lancet Oncology (2025), opens source in new tab

Why surgical approaches differ

Most robot-assisted operations approach the prostate through the abdomen, but other routes spare certain tissues or avoid entering the abdominal cavity. Retzius-sparing surgery preserves structures in front of the bladder. Single-port systems place several instruments through one access site, although the internal operation remains complex.

These approaches may influence early recovery in selected groups. Evidence quality and selection vary, and no specific port arrangement ensures better long-term cancer outcomes. An experienced multidisciplinary team should explain which anatomy and cancer features make a given approach reasonable.

Supporting sources: PLoS ONE (2022), opens source in new tab · European Urology Oncology (2026), opens source in new tab

The anatomy behind recovery

Urinary leakage after surgery can result from temporary changes to sphincter function and support, while erection recovery depends partly on prior function and nerve preservation. Since seminal vesicles are removed, dry ejaculation is expected even if orgasm remains possible. Rehabilitation may help address urinary and sexual consequences.

Learning a few basic terms before a consultation makes the treatment conversation more concrete: bladder neck, external sphincter, neurovascular bundle, apex, surgical margin and anastomosis.

Supporting sources: American Urological Association (2024), opens source in new tab · National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab

Connecting anatomy to real clinical decisions

When a surgeon describes the bladder neck, external sphincter, seminal vesicles, rectum or neurovascular bundles, each structure has a particular role. The urinary sphincter helps control leakage, while the nerves alongside the gland contribute to erection signaling. Preserving tissue can be desirable, but it must not compromise removal of cancer where the margin is at risk.

An anatomical illustration is a model, not a map of an individual patient's tissues. Prostate size, surgical history, tumor location and tissue variation all affect how an operation is performed. Ask which structures the surgical team expects to preserve, what might prevent nerve sparing and how functional outcomes are defined. These questions explain more than the brand of surgical robot.

Questions to bring to your appointment

  • Which parts of my anatomy or cancer location limit nerve sparing?
  • What urinary and sexual functions are most likely to change?
  • Is a particular surgical approach appropriate in my case, and why?
  • How will I know whether recovery is progressing as expected?
