BPH, prostatitis and prostate cancer

Understand three different conditions that can cause overlapping concerns without assuming every urinary symptom is cancer.

Updated November 7, 2026 · Men's health education · Not clinically peer reviewed

Urinary symptoms do not tell you by themselves whether the prostate is enlarged, inflamed or cancerous. The right response is to evaluate the cause rather than treat a symptom list as a diagnostic test.

Key distinction: Symptoms alone cannot distinguish prostate cancer from benign conditions.

Benign enlargement is common

Benign prostatic hyperplasia, or BPH, is noncancerous prostate enlargement. It may narrow the urethra, causing a slower stream, hesitancy, incomplete emptying, nighttime urination or urgency. Symptoms depend on more than gland size, so a large prostate may cause little trouble and a smaller one may cause substantial difficulty.

Clinicians may review symptoms, urine tests, medications, kidney function and the degree of bladder emptying before recommending observation, medication or procedures. BPH and prostate cancer can coexist, but BPH does not mean cancer is present.

Prostatitis has several patterns

Prostatitis means inflammation or a condition involving pain in the prostate/pelvic region. A sudden bacterial infection can produce fever, chills, pelvic pain and urinary difficulties and deserves urgent assessment. Chronic symptoms can have several causes and may not represent a persistent bacterial infection.

Treatment therefore depends on the specific syndrome. Antibiotics are important for bacterial infection but are not a universal solution for every chronic pelvic pain complaint. A clinician may assess the urinary tract and other causes of pelvic pain.

Supporting sources: NIH / NIDDK (2025), opens source in new tab

Cancer may be silent

Many early prostate cancers cause no symptoms. A person can feel well and still have a cancer worth finding. On the other hand, urinary urgency, a weak stream and nocturia often have benign explanations and should not be labeled cancer without testing.

Prostate cancer risk is assessed using age, ancestry, family history, inherited variants, PSA results and other clinical context. Screening decisions are distinct from evaluating symptoms: a new symptom deserves its own assessment even when routine screening is not otherwise being considered.

Supporting sources: National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab · AUA/SUO (2026), opens source in new tab

When to seek prompt help

Seek urgent medical evaluation for inability to pass urine, fever with significant urinary symptoms, severe pain or signs of serious illness. Visible blood in urine also needs medical evaluation. For persistent but nonurgent changes, record when symptoms started, medications, bladder symptoms and any associated pain before an appointment.

The practical goal is not to guess which condition is present. It is to ensure that symptoms, testing and the follow-up plan are connected.

Supporting sources: NIH / NIDDK (2025), opens source in new tab · National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab

Why similar symptoms call for different evaluations

Nighttime urination can arise from benign obstruction, bladder overactivity, sleep disturbances, fluid intake, medicines or other health conditions. Prostatitis and urinary infections can cause pain or burning, while many early prostate cancers are silent. A PSA measurement alone cannot assign the cause of a symptom.

BPH therapy can involve observation, medication or procedures, but prostate cancer treatment follows an entirely different risk assessment. Discussing an intervention for an enlarged gland should include expected symptom improvement, likelihood of repeat treatment, catheter or recovery time, and possible effects on ejaculation or erections. New technology claims should be compared with the best available evidence, not only a promotional success percentage.

Supporting sources: NIH / NIDDK (2025), opens source in new tab · National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab · National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab

Measure urinary symptoms before choosing a treatment

A weak stream, urgency and waking to urinate can be frustrating, but the same symptoms do not always have the same cause. A clinician may ask you to complete the International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS). It covers seven urinary symptoms, producing a score from 0 to 35; a separate question asks how much the symptoms affect daily life. The score is useful for comparing your symptoms over time, not for diagnosing BPH or ruling out cancer on its own.

The evaluation may include a medication review, examination and urine testing. In selected situations, a bladder diary, measurement of urine remaining after you void, or other tests can show whether the main problem is obstruction, bladder behavior or something else. A practical starting point is to bring a record of what happens, how often it happens and what bothers you most. A man with frequent nighttime urination may need a different evaluation from someone who cannot start a stream.

  • A score of 0–7 is generally classified as mild, 8–19 as moderate, and 20–35 as severe; the impact on quality of life still matters.
  • An IPSS number does not measure prostate size or identify prostate cancer.
  • Tell the care team about cold remedies, antihistamines and other medicines that might make urination harder.

Supporting sources: American Urological Association (2023), opens source in new tab · American Urological Association (2026), opens source in new tab · NIH / NIDDK (2026), opens source in new tab

Why common BPH medicines work differently

One group of medicines, called alpha-blockers, relaxes muscle around the prostate and bladder outlet. Examples include tamsulosin and alfuzosin. Some men notice better flow within days or weeks. These drugs do not shrink the prostate, and potential side effects include lightheadedness, changes in blood pressure and altered ejaculation. A history of planned cataract surgery is important to mention when discussing an alpha-blocker.

Another group, 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors, includes finasteride and dutasteride. These reduce conversion of testosterone into dihydrotestosterone (DHT), a hormone involved in prostate tissue growth. In men with an enlarged prostate, they may gradually reduce gland volume and lower the chance of future urinary retention or surgery. Benefits take months rather than days, and sexual side effects can occur. BPH is not simply proof of high testosterone; aging, hormones, prostate structure and bladder function all contribute.

The two medication classes are sometimes prescribed together for men whose symptoms and prostate enlargement make that approach appropriate. Medicines that reduce prostate size also tend to lower PSA measurements, which changes how later results must be interpreted. Anyone considering or already taking these medicines should make sure the clinician following their PSA knows the exact drug and when it was started.

Supporting sources: American Urological Association (2023), opens source in new tab · American Urological Association (2026), opens source in new tab · NIH / NIDDK (2026), opens source in new tab

Where daily tadalafil fits, and when combinations need caution

Tadalafil is best known as an erectile dysfunction medicine, but a prescribed once-daily 5 mg regimen is also an established option for bothersome urinary symptoms of BPH, whether or not erectile dysfunction is present. It works through a different signaling pathway from alpha-blockers and prostate-shrinking drugs; it does not remove enlarged tissue. For a man dealing with both concerns, discussing both goals in one appointment can prevent an incomplete treatment plan.

Tadalafil must not be taken with nitrate medicines used for some heart conditions or with medicines such as riociguat, because blood pressure can fall dangerously. Combining it with an alpha-blocker may also lower blood pressure. The current U.S. Cialis prescribing information specifically does not recommend tadalafil plus an alpha-blocker for treatment of BPH, even though some professional guidance discusses selected combinations. Do not start, stop or combine these medicines without the prescribing clinician checking the indication and the full medication list.

Supporting sources: American Urological Association (2026), opens source in new tab · FDA-approved prescribing information (2026), opens source in new tab

What changes when medication is not enough

Persistent symptoms do not automatically mean surgery is needed. The next step depends on what is causing them, how disruptive they are, how well medicines work and whether there are complications. A urologist may review prostate size and shape, residual urine, bladder function and the effects a procedure might have on ejaculation, erections and recovery.

Procedural options include techniques that remove tissue, reduce tissue volume or widen the urinary channel. They vary in durability, side effects, recovery requirements and the likelihood of needing another treatment. If urinary retention, repeated infection, bladder stones or kidney problems develop, the conversation may become more urgent. BPH procedures are different from radical prostatectomy for cancer, and a new device or less invasive approach is not automatically better for every anatomy or symptom pattern.

Frequently asked questions

Does the size of my prostate predict how bad my urinary symptoms will be?

Not reliably. A relatively small gland can still be associated with significant difficulty, while a larger gland may cause only modest symptoms. Bladder function, muscle tone, medicines and other conditions all influence the experience. A symptom score and appropriate evaluation are more useful than gland size alone.

Supporting sources: NIH / NIDDK (2026), opens source in new tab · American Urological Association (2026), opens source in new tab

What does an IPSS score tell me?

It puts seven common urinary symptoms on a consistent scale so you and your clinician can assess how much trouble you are having and whether care is helping. It is not a cancer test or a diagnosis by itself. The separate quality-of-life question matters because two people with the same score may want different approaches.

Supporting sources: American Urological Association (2023), opens source in new tab · American Urological Association (2026), opens source in new tab

Why can one BPH medicine help quickly while another takes months?

Alpha-blockers ease the muscular tension around the urinary outlet, so some people notice a change relatively soon. Finasteride or dutasteride work on the hormonal process involved in tissue growth, so shrinking an enlarged prostate takes longer. Their uses and side effects differ, and the choice depends on the clinical findings.

Supporting sources: American Urological Association (2023), opens source in new tab · American Urological Association (2026), opens source in new tab · NIH / NIDDK (2026), opens source in new tab

Can tadalafil address both urination problems and erections?

For some men, yes. Daily tadalafil is a recognized treatment option for BPH symptoms as well as erectile dysfunction, but it is not suitable for everyone. Nitrates and riociguat are incompatible with it, and certain combinations with prostate or blood-pressure medicines need particular care. A clinician should review all medications before prescribing it.

Supporting sources: FDA-approved prescribing information (2026), opens source in new tab · American Urological Association (2026), opens source in new tab

Will finasteride or dutasteride affect my PSA tests?

Yes. These medicines commonly lower measured PSA, so a later value should not be compared with older results without accounting for treatment. Tell the clinician interpreting your PSA which medicine you take and when you started it. A lower PSA number on treatment does not itself rule out cancer.

Supporting sources: American Urological Association (2023), opens source in new tab · American Urological Association (2026), opens source in new tab

When do urinary symptoms need prompt medical attention?

Seek urgent care if you cannot pass urine at all. Fever or chills with painful urinary symptoms, visible blood in urine, severe lower abdominal pain or feeling very unwell also call for prompt evaluation. Do not assume those symptoms are simply routine prostate enlargement.

Supporting sources: NIH / NIDDK (2026), opens source in new tab
