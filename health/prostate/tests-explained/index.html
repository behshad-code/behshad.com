Tests & Diagnosis

Prostate tests explained: MRI, biopsy, Grade Group and genomic tools

There isn't one test that answers every prostate cancer question. Some tests ask whether cancer might be present, others establish what the tissue looks like, and still others estimate how the disease may behave.

Think of testing as different questions

Three purposes that should not be confused

A diagnostic test helps establish whether cancer is present. A prognostic test estimates what may happen with a known diagnosis. A predictive test estimates relative benefit from particular treatment choices. A prognostic genomic or image-based risk score should not be treated as a validated instruction to add or omit therapy unless that use has been demonstrated. Genomic and genetic testing explained →

MRI and PI-RADS

Multiparametric MRI is used to identify and characterize suspicious areas, support biopsy planning and help assess disease already diagnosed. A radiology report may describe lesions using PI-RADS, a five-category scale where higher categories reflect greater suspicion for clinically significant prostate cancer.

Ask where a lesion is located, whether it has changed, and whether there are reasons for concern outside the visible area. Some tumors occur in the anterior or transition regions of the gland, where a digital rectal examination alone may not detect them. MRI can improve localization, but an MRI read as low suspicion does not guarantee the absence of important disease.

What a reassuring MRI cannot settle

MRI quality and reader expertise affect interpretation. A low-suspicion PI-RADS result can lower concern but does not rule out every Grade Group 2 or higher cancer. Some centers use abbreviated MRI protocols or micro-ultrasound in selected situations; the appropriate approach depends on the clinical question and local capability. How a prostate biopsy answers a different question

A pathologist examines prostate tissue taken through a transperineal (through the skin between the scrotum and anus) or transrectal route. When MRI reveals suspicious areas, a clinician may discuss targeting those regions and sampling other parts of the gland systematically. The aim is to detect clinically meaningful cancer while reducing the chance of missing it through limited sampling.

The pathology report should identify how many cores contained cancer, how much was present, the highest Grade Group, and any additional features considered relevant. If clinical findings and pathology seem out of step, asking for explanation or an expert pathology review can be reasonable.

Biopsy route and the PREVENT results

A biopsy may be performed through the perineum or rectum. In the final PREVENT randomized trial, no infections occurred among 372 men biopsied transperineally without preventive antibiotics, versus six among 370 men biopsied transrectally with tailored antibiotic prevention. Detection of higher-grade cancer was similar. These trial results do not guarantee an individual risk of zero, and procedure experience differs by center.

Supporting evidence: JAMA Oncology (2024) ↗

3+4 is not the same as 4+3. The predominant pattern is listed first, which is why the order matters. Other details, including the amount of pattern 4 and findings such as cribriform or intraductal patterns, can affect how clinicians interpret risk.

Grade, stage and risk are different

The Grade Group describes microscopic cancer patterns; stage describes where disease is believed to be; a risk category combines grade, PSA, stage and other details. Changes in guideline categories can make older reports use different labels from current recommendations. Learn how grade and stage fit together →

When genomic tests seem to disagree

Commercial tissue-based tests, such as Decipher and other classifiers, may add prognostic information in selected situations. They examine features of tumor tissue and should be interpreted alongside PSA, grade, cancer volume, imaging, stage, health status and the planned treatment. Some newer systems analyze digitized pathology images using AI.

Two methods can reach different risk categories because they use different inputs, training populations, models and endpoints. A label such as “high risk” from one test may not be measuring exactly the same outcome as an estimate from another. Ask the clinician to explain the absolute risk, time horizon and intended clinical use of each result before treating an apparent conflict as an emergency or an error.

Inherited (germline) genetic testing is a separate question: it looks for variants that may affect an individual's risk, family screening or treatment eligibility. A tumor classifier is not the same as an inherited genetic test.

Genomic and inherited genetic information

Decipher and similar tumor-tissue classifiers may add prognostic information, but exploratory treatment-interaction analyses should not be treated as fully established drug-selection rules. Germline genetic tests answer inherited family-risk questions, which are distinct from changes found only in tumor cells. Regulatory authorization of a specific AI prognostic tool does not make its output a treatment mandate.

Supporting evidence: AUA/ASTRO (2026) ↗ · European Association of Urology (2026) ↗

The questions that help make all the reports useful

Bring these to your next consultation

  • What question was each test ordered to answer?
  • Which findings are established facts, and which are risk estimates?
  • Do the MRI and biopsy sample the same suspicious region?
  • What is the highest Grade Group, and what else matters on the pathology report?
  • Would additional testing change the management choice or only add information?
  • If two tests disagree, what clinical evidence is carrying the most weight?
  • Would a second pathology opinion or specialist consultation be worthwhile?

PSMA PET is used for different purposes

PSMA PET has defined roles in staging selected higher-risk disease and assessing suspected recurrence. An initially negative scan, especially at very low PSA, does not exclude microscopic cancer. Experimental studies of PSMA PET before initial biopsy should not be confused with established staging uses. Explore PSMA PET and imaging →
