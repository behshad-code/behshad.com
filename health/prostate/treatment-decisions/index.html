Choosing a prostate cancer treatment: compare the tradeoffs

The goal is not to find the most impressive technology or the loudest recommendation. It is to understand the cancer's actual risk, the realistic choices for that risk group and the tradeoffs that matter to the person facing them.

First understand the risk group

Grade Group, PSA, clinical stage, biopsy volume and selected pathology features are used to classify prostate cancer risk. Imaging and sometimes other tests add context. The treatment conversation for low-risk disease is different from the conversation for unfavorable intermediate- or high-risk disease.

That distinction changes the option set. Active surveillance is often appropriate for low-risk disease. Men with favorable intermediate-risk cancer may discuss surveillance, surgery and radiation. For men with unfavorable intermediate- or high-risk localized disease, definitive approaches such as surgery or radiation often become more prominent, and hormone therapy may be part of a radiation plan.

Clarify the disease state before comparing treatment

A newly localized diagnosis, PSA-only recurrence and metastatic disease require different treatment discussions. Drugs appropriate for PSMA-positive metastatic cancer are not interchangeable with surgery or radiation choices for low-risk localized disease. Start by asking the team to identify disease stage, risk group and intended treatment goal.

Compare the main approaches

ApproachPotential advantagesImportant tradeoffs and questions
Active surveillanceMay avoid or delay urinary, sexual and bowel side effects of treatment when the cancer is suitable.Requires tests, repeat assessments and readiness to act if risk changes.
Radical prostatectomyRemoves the prostate and provides surgical pathology; PSA generally falls to very low or undetectable levels afterward.Surgical and anesthetic risks, catheter/recovery period, potential urinary leakage and erectile difficulties; outcomes vary by patient and surgeon.
External-beam radiationCan treat the prostate without removing it; different schedules and techniques are available.Urinary and bowel irritation, possible later sexual effects, different PSA monitoring, and possible hormone therapy depending on risk.
BrachytherapyInternal radiation delivered to the prostate; can be used alone or combined with external-beam treatment in selected cases.Suitability depends on anatomy, disease risk, urinary function and specialist experience.
Hormone therapy (ADT)May improve outcomes when combined with radiation for certain risk groups; important for some advanced settings.Fatigue, hot flashes, sexual effects and possible metabolic, bone and cardiovascular considerations.
Focal / investigational approachesSeek to treat less tissue in selected circumstances.Long-term evidence and eligibility are less settled; ask if a clinical trial or specialized evaluation is appropriate.

Five-session radiation and its tradeoffs

In the randomized PACE-B trial, five-session SBRT met the predefined criterion for cancer-control noninferiority against longer radiotherapy courses in eligible low/intermediate-risk patients. Five-year freedom from failure was 95.8% versus 94.6%; moderate-or-worse late urinary toxicity was higher with SBRT (26.9% versus 18.3%). PACE-B did not compare radiation with surgery. High-risk combinations require accurate eligibility

The 2026 PROTEUS phase 3 trial enrolled 2,109 men having surgery for high-risk localized or locally advanced cancer. Both arms received ADT; adding apalutamide increased five-year metastasis-free survival from 73.5% to 78.2%, while grade 3 or 4 adverse events rose from 31.0% to 39.6%. These findings do not justify routine use in low-risk disease. Understand hormone and combination treatment →

Focal approaches need long-term context

Treatments such as irreversible electroporation seek to preserve tissue, but long-term comparative outcomes are less established. NICE recommends IRE for prostate cancer only with special governance, informed consent, and audit or research arrangements. Marketing about a quick 'cure' is not evidence of durable disease control. Why two good specialists can frame the same choice differently

A urologic surgeon and a radiation oncologist work with different procedures and may emphasize different strengths. A consultation with each is valuable when both approaches are realistic options. That is not a reason to distrust specialists; it is a reason to understand the full menu before making an irreversible decision.

When clinicians offer different recommendations, ask each to identify exactly which clinical facts led to that recommendation. A useful comparison covers cancer control, short- and long-term side effects, effect on daily life, follow-up testing, and what could be done if the first treatment does not achieve its goal.

Hearing from another patient can help make side effects and recovery feel more concrete, but one person's experience cannot predict another person's outcome. Clinical facts and personal priorities still have to guide the decision.

Choose the team, not just the robot

The randomized Brisbane robotic-versus-open comparison found similar urinary and sexual function at two years. Surgical approach, nerve-sparing decisions, tumor risk and team experience are more useful questions than a model name alone. The NeuroSAFE trial illustrates how a selected technique may improve function while maintaining attention to margins. Explore robotic surgery →

Ask for a side-by-side discussion of what happens afterward

  • After surgery: the removed tissue provides a more complete pathology assessment. PSA is monitored after recovery; detecting a rising trend may lead to discussions of salvage therapy.
  • After radiation: the prostate remains in place and PSA usually declines toward a nadir over time. Interpretation of later PSA changes differs from post-surgery monitoring.
  • With either approach: cancer control is not the only outcome that matters. Baseline urinary, bowel and sexual function, existing conditions, and quality-of-life priorities should be discussed before treatment.

Cancer recurrence creates different decisions

After local treatment, a confirmed PSA trend may lead to salvage-therapy discussion even when imaging is negative. For eligible PSMA-positive metastatic patients, the FDA expanded Pluvicto's approved treatment use in July 2026; that indication does not apply to a newly localized diagnosis. The questions that make consultations more useful

Questions to ask both teams

  • What is my risk classification and which findings drive it?
  • What are the reasonable choices for someone with these findings?
  • What is the likely cancer-control benefit for me, and how certain is that estimate?
  • How might this affect continence, erections, bowel function and everyday life?
  • For surgery: who performs the critical steps, and is nerve preservation appropriate?
  • For radiation: which technique, how many sessions, and would hormone therapy be recommended?
  • What is the follow-up plan and how would recurrence be recognized?
  • What salvage options would remain if the initial treatment were not enough?
  • Would a second opinion change the information we have or help clarify the choice?

Ask for comparable outcomes

Request each specialist's best estimate of cancer control and urinary, bowel and sexual outcomes with their recommended plan, including meaningful differences in study populations and follow-up. If surgery is being considered, ask about nerve sparing, the operating surgeon's experience and margin risk. Printable surgeon questions →
