The journey

Recovery happens in phases.

No fixed date predicts when everyone will regain bladder control, sexual function or normal energy. Use these periods as prompts for your care team.

BEFORE SURGERYPrepare and document

Confirm your procedure, baseline urinary and sexual function, medicines, help at home and who to call.

DAYS 0–14Heal and manage the catheter

Follow your discharge instructions for catheter care, walking, incisions, fluids and bowel function. Your team sets removal timing.

AFTER CATHETER TO ~6 WEEKSAdjust to leakage and activity

Use appropriate protection, track symptoms, and confirm when pelvic-floor work, driving and lifting may resume.

MONTHS 2–3Build healthy routines

Review pathology and PSA follow-up, physical recovery and any need for rehabilitation referrals.

MONTHS 3–6Check your progress

Discuss urinary urgency or stress leakage and treatment choices for erectile difficulties.

MONTHS 6–12Address lingering concerns

Persistent, bothersome leakage and sexual problems deserve a specialist conversation, not endless waiting.

YEAR 1, YEAR 2 AND BEYONDRebuild quality of life

Continue appropriate PSA surveillance, treat ongoing problems, support emotional and relationship health, and return to sustainable fitness.

Do not ignore warning signs

Contact your care team promptly for fever, wound changes, blocked catheter, inability to urinate, worsening pain or new one-sided leg swelling. Seek emergency help for chest pain, sudden severe breathing difficulty, collapse or coughing up blood. In the U.S., call 911 for emergencies.

Regain function

Three recovery tracks. Different timelines.

Physical healing

Walk as advised, balance rest and activity, manage constipation and protect internal healing. Return to work, exercise and lifting depends on your procedure and clinician's clearance.

Urinary control

Leakage may increase after catheter removal. Learn proper pelvic-floor contractions and relaxation. Compare trends in pads, urgency and daily impact, not a single day.

Sexual recovery

Even after nerve-sparing surgery, erections may take longer to return or may not recover fully. Treatments can help sexual activity, but no regimen guarantees spontaneous nerve recovery.

If urinary leakage continues

  • Is it leakage with coughing, activity or standing, or urgency before the toilet?
  • Would a pelvic-health physical therapist help evaluate your technique?
  • Do persistent symptoms warrant a continence specialist?

If sexual function changes

  • Ask about erection medicines, vacuum devices, injections and specialist care.
  • Orgasm may remain possible without semen ejaculation.
  • Discuss sensation, intimacy, confidence and relationship support.

A useful six-month and one-year discussion

For persistent bothersome stress incontinence, the AUA guideline allows consideration of surgery as early as six months if conservative care is not helping and recommends offering surgical treatment at one year when symptoms persist. It does not mean surgery is right for everyone.

Life after surgery

Recovery does not end at one year.

Some function can keep changing into the second year, while certain effects can persist. A good survivorship plan addresses cancer monitoring and daily quality of life together.

Your ongoing health plan

  • Know the final pathology findings and exact PSA monitoring schedule.
  • Identify which clinician manages urinary, sexual and emotional issues.
  • Build back physical activity and strength under appropriate guidance.
  • Choose balanced nutrition; be skeptical of supplements promising recovery or cancer control.

Questions worth bringing

  • Which recovery changes are expected for my procedure and baseline health?
  • Who should I call if a symptom worries me?
  • When should I request pelvic-floor or sexual-medicine care?
  • What options remain if urinary or erectile problems persist?

Make your follow-up personal

Track what matters to you: pads used, urgency, comfort, energy, activity, erections, intimacy and how symptoms affect everyday life. Discuss changes with your clinical team rather than comparing your recovery with someone else's.
