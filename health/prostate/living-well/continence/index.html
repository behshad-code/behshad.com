Urinary continence and pelvic-floor recovery

What recovery statistics mean, when pelvic-floor care may help, and what to do if leakage persists.

Updated November 9, 2026 · Men's health education · Not clinically peer reviewed

Urinary leakage after radical prostatectomy is common, particularly after catheter removal. Most men improve, but the pace and final outcome vary considerably, and persistent leakage should not simply be ignored.

Key distinction: There is no reliable universal month-by-month recovery timetable.

What early recovery may look like

The urinary sphincter, bladder neck and supporting structures are affected by prostate removal. Leakage can be more noticeable on standing, coughing or exercising, and recovery often takes months. The type of surgery, age, baseline bladder function and other conditions influence the course.

A reported continence rate is incomplete without a definition. 'No pads' differs from 'one precautionary pad' and from questionnaire-based scores. Some highly selected robotic cohorts report encouraging early results, but these should not be presented as a personal prediction.

Pelvic-floor muscle training

A therapist specializing in pelvic health can teach correct pelvic-floor contraction and relaxation, and help avoid excessive or ineffective exercises. Some teams advise learning the technique before surgery and resuming exercises at an appropriate point afterward.

Ask your surgeon when pelvic-floor therapy is safe and suitable, and whether urinary urgency, bladder overactivity or other causes should be evaluated. A generic Kegel schedule is not appropriate for every recovery stage.

Supporting sources: American Urological Association (2024), opens source in new tab · National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab

What techniques may influence recovery

Retzius-sparing and other tissue-preserving techniques may accelerate early continence in selected patients. Longer-term advantages are less certain and must be considered alongside cancer clearance. No access route or reconstruction can guarantee pad-free recovery.

Studies vary in how long patients are followed and how they define improvement. The surgical team's experience and your individual circumstances often matter more than one headline percentage.

Supporting sources: PLoS ONE (2022), opens source in new tab · European Urology Oncology (2026), opens source in new tab

When leakage continues

Persistent or distressing leakage deserves assessment rather than indefinite reassurance. Options can include specialized physical therapy, medication for certain symptoms, continence devices or procedures in appropriately selected patients.

Describe how many pads are used, what triggers leakage, whether urgency accompanies it and how daily life is affected. A urology continence specialist can explain benefits, harms and timing of further treatment.

Supporting sources: American Urological Association (2024), opens source in new tab · National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab

Choosing meaningful urinary recovery measures

Ask how the treating team defines continence before comparing its outcome statistics with another clinic. No pads, one security pad and questionnaire-based leakage scores are not interchangeable. Recovery reports can also exclude patients with baseline leakage or use different follow-up periods. Keeping a symptom diary describing pads, urgency, exertional leakage and quality-of-life effects helps a specialist identify what remains bothersome.

Persistent leakage deserves a structured assessment, because stress incontinence, urgency and incomplete emptying may require different approaches. Pelvic-floor therapy is not simply a fixed number of contractions; instruction should account for correct technique and relaxation. Continued symptoms may warrant evaluation for devices, medications or procedural options in selected patients rather than indefinite waiting.

Supporting sources: American Urological Association (2024), opens source in new tab · Lancet Oncology (2018), opens source in new tab · National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab

A practical plan for regaining bladder control

Leakage after radical prostatectomy is not one single problem. Stress incontinence means urine escapes with movement, lifting, standing or coughing. Urgency incontinence involves a sudden compelling need to urinate, sometimes before reaching a toilet. Both may occur together, and symptoms such as weak flow or difficulty emptying require clinical assessment rather than more pelvic-floor exercises by default.

How to build a useful continence routine

  1. Learn the correct muscles. Ask a pelvic-health physical therapist or the surgical team to confirm that the pelvic floor contracts and fully relaxes correctly. Avoid routinely practicing by stopping urine flow, and do not strain your abdomen or hold your breath during exercises.
  2. Follow your team's start date. Some men learn pelvic-floor technique before surgery. Ask when to resume after surgery and catheter removal; a generic repetition schedule cannot replace individualized coaching.
  3. Observe what changes. Track pads or guards used, leakage with activity, urgency, nighttime symptoms and how bothersome they are. The amount of leakage matters more than pad count alone.
  4. Protect skin and daily life. Use properly fitting absorbent products if needed, change them when wet and discuss irritation or skin breakdown with a clinician. Pads help manage symptoms while function improves; they are not a treatment for the cause.

When progress stalls

Do not assume persistent leakage must simply be tolerated. Assessment can distinguish sphincter-related stress leakage from overactive bladder or other causes. Depending on findings, treatment may include individualized physiotherapy, behavioral approaches, medication for urgency symptoms, or procedures such as a male sling or artificial urinary sphincter for selected stress incontinence cases.

The AUA/GURS/SUFU guideline states that surgery may be offered for bothersome stress incontinence as early as six months if it is not improving despite conservative therapy, and that surgical treatment should be offered to appropriate people at one year if bothersome stress incontinence persists. These are points for discussion, not automatic deadlines or guaranteed outcomes.

Guideline: AUA/GURS/SUFU, Incontinence After Prostate Treatment (amended 2024). See the full surgery recovery timeline for how this fits into the first year.

Questions to bring to your appointment

  • How does this team define successful continence?
  • When should I meet a pelvic-floor specialist?
  • Which changes are expected and which need evaluation?
  • What options exist if leakage remains troublesome?
