Sexual health and intimacy after treatment

Erections, orgasm, ejaculation, relationships and how to discuss meaningful outcomes.

Updated November 7, 2026 · Men's health education · Not clinically peer reviewed

Sexual recovery is more than whether an erection returns. Prostate cancer treatment can change desire, orgasm, sensation, semen, body image, intimacy and relationships in different ways.

Key distinction: Outcomes depend on baseline function, age, treatment and patient-defined priorities.

Surgery and radiation affect function differently

Nerves involved in erection lie near the prostate and may be affected by surgery, even when nerve-sparing is attempted. Recovery may be gradual, sometimes over many months, and depends on previous function, health and treatment details.

Radiation can affect blood vessels, nerves or surrounding structures over time, while hormone therapy commonly changes libido and sexual function. Discuss likely effects before treatment so that goals and support can be built into the plan.

Orgasm, ejaculation and other concerns

After radical prostatectomy, the prostate and seminal vesicles are removed, so semen is no longer ejaculated even when orgasm remains possible. Some men experience changes in orgasm sensation, urinary leakage during sexual activity or penile shortening.

Different sexual practices, partners, orientations and personal priorities can change which outcomes matter most. A respectful consultation should ask what is important to the person, rather than assuming erectile firmness is the only meaningful measure.

Supporting sources: National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab · American Urological Association (2024), opens source in new tab

Supportive options

Depending on circumstances, clinicians may discuss PDE5 inhibitors, vacuum devices, injections, implants, pelvic-floor therapy, counseling or specialist sexual-medicine care. Benefits and safety differ by medical history and timing.

Nerve-sparing surgery does not guarantee that any particular intervention will restore erections. Beware of commercial therapies marketed as instant or certain recovery without strong clinical evidence.

Supporting sources: National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab · AUA/ASTRO (2026), opens source in new tab

Relationships and mental well-being

A change in sexual function can affect confidence, closeness and communication. Couples counseling, mental-health services or peer support can be valuable when desired. People without partners also deserve care that acknowledges sexual well-being.

A practical starting point is to discuss desired outcomes early with the treatment team and ask for a referral before frustration or distress becomes entrenched.

Supporting sources: PCFA (2026), opens source in new tab · National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab

Define the outcomes that matter before and after treatment

Erectile hardness, ability to have partnered sexual activity, orgasm, sensation, ejaculation and fertility are distinct outcomes. A patient may regain one function without another. Surgery, radiation and hormone therapy affect them differently, and baseline sexual function is important when interpreting later measurements. After radical prostatectomy, ejaculation of semen does not return, although orgasmic sensation may remain.

Rehabilitation options can include a discussion of oral medicines, vacuum devices, injections, specialized counseling and, for some patients, other interventions. Decisions should reflect medical suitability, preferences, partner circumstances and uncertainty about long-term effectiveness. Ask the team about realistic time frames and whether they can provide inclusive sexual-health care that addresses the person's actual practices and priorities.

How erection medicines fit into prostate treatment recovery

After prostate cancer treatment, changes in erectile function may reflect effects on nerves, blood vessels, hormone levels or a combination of factors. Sildenafil and tadalafil belong to a group called PDE5 inhibitors. They help the natural blood-flow response to sexual stimulation; they do not create desire or automatically produce an erection without arousal.

These medicines can make sexual activity possible for some men during recovery, but response depends on baseline erectile function, age, the treatment received and the extent of nerve or vascular injury. It is important not to confuse an erection achieved with medication with proof that the underlying nerves have fully recovered. Research has not established that taking a PDE5 inhibitor early after surgery reliably restores spontaneous, unassisted erections later. Recovery goals and other options belong in a discussion with the treating team.

Supporting sources: American Urological Association (2018), opens source in new tab · National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab

Sildenafil and tadalafil: the practical differences

The important difference is not that one medicine is universally stronger. Sildenafil is usually taken before anticipated sexual activity and has a shorter period of effect. Tadalafil can remain active for as long as about 36 hours and is also available in a daily prescribed schedule. The longer window does not mean a continuous erection. Either medicine still relies on sexual stimulation.

A high-fat meal may delay sildenafil's absorption and the time it takes to work; tadalafil is less affected by food. Side effects overlap, including headache, flushing, congestion and indigestion. Some people experience back or muscle discomfort with tadalafil or temporary visual effects with sildenafil. Individual response, other medicines, kidney or liver health, and preferences matter more than a generic ranking of the two. They should not be combined or used more often than prescribed.

Supporting sources: NIH / DailyMed (2025), opens source in new tab · FDA-approved prescribing information (2026), opens source in new tab · American Urological Association (2018), opens source in new tab

Safety questions worth asking before taking an ED medicine

PDE5 inhibitors must not be taken with nitrate drugs or nitrate-containing recreational substances, or with riociguat. Such combinations can cause a dangerous drop in blood pressure. Other drugs, including alpha-blockers used for BPH and some blood-pressure treatments, require a careful medication review because of additive blood-pressure effects. Tell every clinician treating chest pain or a medical emergency when you last used an ED medicine.

Seek urgent medical care for an erection lasting more than four hours or sudden loss of sight or hearing. Heart symptoms during sexual activity also require medical assessment. For men with significant cardiovascular disease, a clinician should assess whether sexual activity and the proposed medicine are appropriate. Do not make medication changes based only on a website comparison.

Supporting sources: FDA-approved prescribing information (2026), opens source in new tab · NIH / DailyMed (2025), opens source in new tab · American Urological Association (2018), opens source in new tab

When erection changes deserve a wider health conversation

Erectile dysfunction is not always solely a sexual problem. It can be associated with blood-vessel health, diabetes, smoking, high blood pressure, medication effects, low mood and other conditions. Professional guidance treats erectile dysfunction as a possible marker of cardiovascular risk. A change that began before cancer treatment, or seems different from what the treatment team expected, may justify a broader health review rather than simply trying a different tablet.

Low sexual desire and difficulty getting or maintaining an erection are different concerns. Hormone treatment for cancer can affect desire, while surgery and radiation may affect physical erectile response. The right evaluation depends on the actual symptom and treatment history. A sexual-medicine specialist can help when tablets are ineffective or unsuitable, alongside the rehabilitation, device and counseling options already discussed in this guide.

Frequently asked questions

If a tablet helps me get an erection, does that mean the nerves have recovered?

Not necessarily. A medication-assisted erection shows that a blood-flow response can occur with help; it does not prove complete nerve recovery. Spontaneous erections and erections supported by a medicine are different outcomes, especially after prostatectomy.

Supporting sources: American Urological Association (2018), opens source in new tab · National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab

Why might sildenafil or tadalafil not work after prostate surgery?

The medicine still needs enough functioning nerve and blood-vessel response to support an erection. Recovery varies, and correct use, sexual stimulation, timing and other health conditions matter. The care team can check for a treatable issue and discuss alternatives rather than assuming that one unsuccessful attempt settles the question.

Supporting sources: American Urological Association (2018), opens source in new tab · National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab

Is tadalafil better than sildenafil?

Neither is the best choice for everyone. Tadalafil lasts longer and is less affected by meals; sildenafil has a shorter window. Effectiveness and side effects vary from person to person. Your other medications, recovery goals and health history should drive the decision.

Supporting sources: NIH / DailyMed (2025), opens source in new tab · FDA-approved prescribing information (2026), opens source in new tab

Can I take an ED medicine if I also take a prostate or heart medicine?

It depends on the exact medicine. Nitrates and riociguat must not be used with PDE5 inhibitors. Some BPH medicines, particularly alpha-blockers, can increase the risk of low blood pressure when combined. Show your complete prescription, over-the-counter and supplement list to the prescriber before taking an ED medication.

Supporting sources: FDA-approved prescribing information (2026), opens source in new tab · NIH / DailyMed (2025), opens source in new tab

Could erectile dysfunction be a warning sign of a heart problem?

It can be a marker of cardiovascular risk, although it does not by itself diagnose heart disease. Blood pressure, diabetes, smoking and cholesterol can be relevant. Following prostate treatment, nerve injury or hormone therapy may also explain changes; a clinician can help separate these possibilities.

Supporting sources: American Urological Association (2018), opens source in new tab

Are low libido and erectile dysfunction the same problem?

No. Libido describes sexual interest; erectile function describes the physical ability to get or maintain an erection. A person may experience one change without the other. Prostate surgery, radiation, cancer hormone therapy, medications and emotional factors can affect these domains differently, so it helps to describe the specific concern.

Supporting sources: National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab · American Urological Association (2018), opens source in new tab

  • What sexual changes are common with the proposed treatment?
  • Which functions may return and which changes are permanent?
  • Can I meet a sexual-medicine specialist before treatment?
  • What resources are available for relationship and emotional concerns?
