Surgery & Recovery

Radical prostatectomy explained, from preparing for surgery and the first weeks at home to regaining function and life afterward.

Updated November 9, 2026 · Men's health education · Not clinically peer reviewed
Download the four-page quick guide ↓Explore the recovery timeline

Recovery from surgery is more than healing an incision. Urinary control, sexual function, physical energy, relationships and follow-up care often progress at different speeds. This compass follows the experience from preparation through the first two years and beyond.

Scope: This guide focuses on radical prostatectomy for prostate cancer, not simple prostatectomy or TURP for an enlarged prostate. The milestones are orientation points, not medical clearance dates. Your surgical team's instructions always take priority.

01 / THE ANATOMY

Understanding radical prostatectomy

The prostate sits below the bladder and surrounds the first portion of the urethra. In radical prostatectomy, the prostate and seminal vesicles are removed, and the bladder is joined to the remaining urethra. Nearby nerves and urinary-support structures can be affected even when nerve sparing is attempted.

02 / THE RECOVERY TRACKS

Three recovery tracks, three different clocks

01

Physical healing

Incisions, fatigue, mobility and return to activity often improve over weeks. The team determines your restrictions.

02

Urinary control

Leakage may start after catheter removal and improve across months. Persistent symptoms deserve evaluation.

03

Sexual function

Changes in erections, orgasm and intimacy may last longer. Nerve-sparing does not guarantee recovery.

These are different outcomes. A person may be back at work but still use pads, or have good urinary control while erections remain impaired.

03 / THE ROADMAP

Your recovery, phase by phase

Use this timeline to frame conversations with your care team. The date ranges overlap because real recovery is not linear.

01
Before surgery

Build a recovery plan

Ask how the procedure may affect your urinary control and sexual function. Document baseline symptoms, prepare your home and clarify who to call after discharge.

  • Review medications and any exercise or pelvic-floor preparation with your team.
  • Ask what catheter supplies are provided and when removal will be assessed.
  • Agree on an initial plan for pathology, PSA testing and rehabilitation.
Practical pre-surgery preparation →
02
Surgery through roughly 2 weeks

Protect healing and manage the catheter

Incisions and the bladder-to-urethra connection begin healing. Temporary fatigue, soreness, bloating and bruising may occur. Many patients go home with a catheter.

  • Learn catheter drainage, cleaning and bag positioning from the discharge team.
  • Walk and gradually move as advised; avoid straining and follow individualized lifting restrictions.
  • Know the after-hours number before leaving the hospital. Catheter removal is scheduled by your surgical team.
How the operation works →
03
After catheter removal through about 6 weeks

Adjust to urinary changes

Leakage can become obvious when the catheter comes out. Recovery often involves pads, gradually increasing activity and learning how to coordinate pelvic-floor muscles.

  • Record pad use, triggers and how much symptoms interfere with daily life.
  • Ask when and how to resume pelvic-floor exercises; seek specialist instruction if unsure.
  • Review driving, work, lifting and exercise restrictions with your clinician.
Continence strategies and pelvic-floor care →
04
Months 2–3

Establish repeatable routines

Physical strength may continue returning while urinary control and sexual response follow their own timelines. Follow-up is about both cancer control and quality of life.

  • Review the final pathology report and establish your personalized PSA schedule.
  • Discuss bladder urgency, leakage and fatigue rather than assuming all symptoms are the same.
  • Ask when a sexual-medicine consultation or rehabilitation discussion would be useful.
Pathology and PSA follow-up →
05
Months 3–6

Review progress without comparing timelines

Improvements may be uneven. Some people regain urinary control earlier, while others need more time or targeted care. Erectile recovery may be considerably slower.

  • Compare your own pad and symptom records over time, not just one good or bad day.
  • Seek evaluation for persistent or bothersome urinary symptoms.
  • Discuss medically appropriate sexual-function treatment options and uncertainty about spontaneous nerve recovery.
Sexual recovery, nerve function and intimacy →
06
Months 6–12

Act on persistent difficulties

A year is a useful reassessment point, not a universal deadline. Some people benefit from additional continence treatment or specialist sexual-health care.

  • Ask whether persistent stress leakage warrants evaluation by a continence specialist.
  • Review whether current erection treatments are helping and what alternatives exist.
  • Continue PSA surveillance according to your individual cancer risk and care plan.
What happens when leakage continues →
07
Year 1, year 2 and beyond

Reclaim long-term quality of life

Some sexual function can continue improving over an extended period, but recovery is not guaranteed. Effective care may involve addressing persistent symptoms, fitness, relationships and emotional health.

  • Build a survivorship plan covering ongoing PSA results and who owns each referral.
  • Treat persistent leakage, erectile dysfunction or distress as legitimate concerns.
  • Maintain movement, balanced nutrition and preventive health care; do not rely on unproven recovery supplements.

04 / SAFETY

Know when to get help

Some soreness, bruising, temporary swelling and mild changes in urine can occur after surgery, but new or worsening symptoms need assessment. Follow the specific thresholds and instructions on your discharge paperwork.

Contact your surgical team promptly

  • Fever, chills, increasing redness or drainage at an incision.
  • Catheter stops draining, causes significant pain, or becomes dislodged.
  • You cannot urinate after catheter removal.
  • Worsening abdominal or pelvic pain, increasing bleeding, or persistent vomiting.
  • New one-sided leg swelling, pain or tenderness.

Seek emergency help

  • Sudden or severe shortness of breath.
  • Chest pain, collapse, or coughing up blood.
  • Severe symptoms or rapid deterioration.

In the United States, call 911 for emergencies.
