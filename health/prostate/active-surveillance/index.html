Monitoring & Risk

Active surveillance is an active plan, not passive waiting

Active surveillance can spare selected men the side effects of immediate treatment. Its safety depends on proper selection, dependable follow-up and willingness to reassess if the evidence changes.

Active surveillance versus watchful waiting

Active surveillance monitors a diagnosed, generally lower-risk prostate cancer with the intent to offer treatment if findings suggest more significant disease. Watchful waiting often focuses on symptoms and quality of life rather than testing for changes that might trigger curative treatment. They are not interchangeable strategies.

For many men with low-risk disease, active surveillance is a preferred option. Some men with favorable intermediate-risk disease may also be candidates after careful evaluation. A decision cannot be based on Grade Group alone; disease volume, PSA density, biopsy features, age, competing health risks and individual priorities all matter.

Who can consider surveillance?

Active surveillance is a standard option for appropriately selected low-risk prostate cancer, and may be discussed in carefully selected favorable intermediate-risk cases. Grade, biopsy volume, MRI, PSA density, health and readiness for follow-up all contribute. Not everyone with Grade Group 2 qualifies.

What a real surveillance plan contains

PSA follow-up

Documented dates and a method for addressing an unexpected result. A one-time change commonly warrants confirmation before major decisions.

Clinical review

Scheduled discussion of results, symptoms and any change in overall health or preferences.

MRI when appropriate

An aid to understanding suspicious lesions or changes, not a universal substitute for tissue sampling.

Repeat biopsy

An agreed approach to confirm initial risk and reassess grade over time, personalized to the case and clinician's protocol.

Triggers to reconsider

Clarity about what new findings would lead to repeat imaging, tissue sampling, an additional opinion or discussion of treatment.

The exact intervals should come from the treating team. A personalized plan should have dates, a clinician responsible for reviewing results, and an explanation of what would change the recommendation.

A written plan is more useful than a universal calendar

A practical plan specifies PSA checks, examinations, MRI use, biopsy timing, who is tracking each result and what would lead to reclassification. Intervals vary with individual findings and local guidelines. A stable scan alone is generally not a reason to abandon indicated repeat biopsy.

What can change the picture

Reasons to revisit surveillance may include confirmed serial PSA increases, concerning changes on MRI, new examination findings or pathology showing higher-grade or greater-volume disease. PSA kinetics alone are imperfect: transient rises happen, and a biopsy finding may carry more weight than a trend.

When a higher Grade Group appears, the amount and kind of new pattern matter. Clinicians may also consider cribriform or intraductal features, PSA density, genomic information when useful, family history and life expectancy. There is no universal rule that one additional data point automatically requires surgery or radiation.

Tracking changes between MRIs

Radiologists may use PRECISE scores when comparing serial prostate MRI: 1–2 can indicate regression, 3 stability, and 4–5 radiological progression. A 2026 multicentre retrospective validation included 1,667 men; the score is one element of monitoring and does not independently replace pathology or the rest of a surveillance plan.

Supporting evidence: European Radiology (2026) ↗

Four misunderstandings to avoid

Common assumptionWhat to understand instead
“No symptoms means nothing is changing.”Localized cancer may produce no urinary symptoms.
“The MRI looks stable, so no other monitoring is necessary.”MRI is useful, but it cannot always replace biopsies in a surveillance protocol.
“My PSA rose, so the cancer has definitely progressed.”PSA can fluctuate for noncancer reasons; clinicians assess the full picture.
“Surveillance means treatment is off the table.”It is a deliberate strategy to defer treatment while preserving options if risk changes.

New markers do not automatically replace biopsy

Researchers are evaluating urine biomarkers that may help select patients for repeat biopsy, but promising discrimination in an observational or validation study does not demonstrate long-term safety when biopsies are skipped. The older ProtecT trial's active-monitoring protocol also differs from contemporary active surveillance.

Supporting evidence: New England Journal of Medicine (2023) ↗ · AUA/ASTRO (2026) ↗

What to ask before choosing surveillance, and at every follow-up

Questions worth asking

  • What features make this cancer suitable for active surveillance?
  • What was sampled and could a more significant focus have been missed?
  • What are my PSA, MRI and repeat-biopsy schedules?
  • What results would prompt us to reassess rather than simply repeat the same plan?
  • If the grade or volume changes, what options would we reconsider?
  • Who tracks the next test and contacts me if it is missed?
  • How does my individual biology or overall health affect this recommendation?

Practical takeaway: Leaving with a written follow-up plan and a way to obtain the complete reports is more useful than leaving with the vague message that the cancer is “slow growing.”

Make the plan actionable

Ask what finding would trigger another MRI or biopsy, what degree of grade or volume change would lead to discussing treatment and who will coordinate tests between visits. Choose questions for your next surveillance visit →
