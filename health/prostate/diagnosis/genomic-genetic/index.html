Genomic, genetic and AI risk tests explained

How tissue classifiers, inherited genetic tests and image-based risk tools differ.

Updated November 6, 2026 · Independent educational writing · Not clinically peer reviewed

Two tests may give different risk impressions without either being wrong. They can measure different aspects of prostate cancer and were often developed for different decisions.

Key distinction: No genomic or AI score should be treated as a stand-alone treatment directive.

Diagnostic, prognostic and predictive mean different things

A diagnostic test helps determine whether cancer is present. A prognostic test estimates an outcome such as recurrence or metastasis risk for someone with a known diagnosis. A predictive test estimates whether a particular treatment is likely to help more than another.

A classifier that is prognostic does not automatically demonstrate which drug or radiation regimen should be chosen. Treatment-selection claims require evidence that using the score to guide care improves the relevant outcome.

Tissue-based genomic classifiers

Tests such as Decipher analyze patterns of gene expression in prostate tumor tissue. They may add prognostic information to Grade Group, PSA and stage in selected circumstances. Whether ordering one changes a decision depends on the clinical situation and guideline context.

Exploratory analyses may suggest that groups with different classifier scores have different treatment benefits. Such findings are not the same as a prospectively validated instruction to intensify or omit treatment. Ask exactly which result, population and outcome the report predicts.

Supporting sources: AUA/ASTRO (2026), opens source in new tab · National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab

Inherited genetics answers a family question

Germline genetic testing seeks inherited variants such as certain BRCA2 changes; tumor sequencing looks at acquired cancer mutations. They may inform different decisions. A tumor test does not replace counseling when an inherited predisposition is suspected.

A genetic counselor can explain family implications, uncertain variants and potential next steps. A broad gene panel can discover changes of uncertain significance that should not trigger unsupported medical actions.

Supporting sources: European Association of Urology (2026), opens source in new tab · National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab

AI tools need precise claims

AI-based analysis of MRI or pathology can assist professionals with detection or risk estimation. Regulatory authorization, when granted, applies to a specific product, population, purpose and output. It does not certify that the AI can select every cancer treatment or outperform every specialist.

Ask whether the tool is available as routine clinical care, what evidence supports its intended use, and how the treating team combines it with conventional pathology and imaging.

Supporting sources: AUA/ASTRO (2026), opens source in new tab · American College of Radiology (2025), opens source in new tab

The claim a new genomic or AI test must support

Diagnostic tools attempt to detect a condition; prognostic tools estimate what may happen; predictive tools identify who is more likely to benefit from a particular intervention. A classifier that separates higher- and lower-risk patients has not necessarily demonstrated that using its score to choose radiation, surgery or a new drug improves outcomes. The strength of the claim depends on prospective validation and clinical utility.

AI models may help clinicians interpret MRI or pathology, but performance against an image dataset does not prove a patient will live longer or avoid harmful treatment. Ask what comparison was used, whether there was external validation across institutions and patient groups, and whether the proposed test supplies information not already explained by Grade Group, PSA, stage and established genetic findings.

Supporting sources: AUA/ASTRO (2026), opens source in new tab · National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab · European Association of Urology (2026), opens source in new tab

Questions to bring to your appointment

  • Is this test diagnostic, prognostic or predictive?
  • What decision could change because of the result?
  • Has the proposed use been tested in people like me?
  • Does the result have implications for relatives?
