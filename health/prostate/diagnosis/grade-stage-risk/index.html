Gleason, Grade Group, stage and risk

The difference between tumor grade, how far cancer has spread, and the overall risk category.

Updated November 6, 2026 · Independent educational writing · Not clinically peer reviewed

Prostate cancer is not one diagnosis with one treatment. The biopsy grade, PSA, clinical stage and extent of disease determine whether monitoring or treatment is most appropriate.

Key distinction: Risk-group names and definitions differ between guideline editions.

Grade measures how cells look

A pathologist examines biopsy tissue and reports Gleason patterns. The Grade Group system translates pattern combinations into five categories. Grade Group 1 is the lowest, while higher groups generally indicate more aggressive-looking disease.

Grade is not the same as stage. A biopsy samples parts of the gland, so the apparent grade can sometimes change after another biopsy or prostatectomy. The number of positive cores and the amount of cancer in each core may also matter.

Stage measures where cancer is

Clinical stage describes what can be inferred from examination, MRI and other tests. Cancer confined to the prostate differs from cancer extending outside the capsule, reaching nearby tissues or spreading to lymph nodes and distant organs.

Imaging is selected for the risk group and treatment question. PSMA PET may improve staging in selected higher-risk situations, but a normal scan does not guarantee the absence of microscopic disease.

Supporting sources: European Association of Urology (2026), opens source in new tab · The Lancet (2020), opens source in new tab

Risk groups combine signals

Clinical risk groups use several findings together, often including PSA, Grade Group, clinical stage and biopsy volume. Guidelines can revise category definitions over time; an older report's label may not align exactly with the current version.

The point of a risk group is to inform options, not to reduce a person to one number. Some low-risk cancers can be actively monitored, whereas other cases benefit from treatment or additional staging. Even within a category, patient health and preferences matter.

Supporting sources: AUA/ASTRO (2026), opens source in new tab · European Association of Urology (2026), opens source in new tab

Making reports actionable

A productive consultation reviews the pathology report alongside MRI, PSA history and any additional tests. Ask the team to identify what remains uncertain and whether another review or second opinion could change management.

If a report contains unfamiliar terms such as perineural invasion, cribriform pattern, intraductal carcinoma or positive margins, ask what each means in the specific context rather than interpreting it alone.

Supporting sources: National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab · AUA/ASTRO (2026), opens source in new tab

How to read the most important lines of a pathology report

Start with the highest reported Grade Group, number of positive cores, cancer involvement in those cores and any additional adverse histological features your clinician considers important. A small sampled amount of higher-grade disease can carry different implications from a large amount of lower-grade disease. The absence of a concerning feature in sampled tissue does not establish that it is absent everywhere in the prostate.

Stage describes the estimated location and extent, which may incorporate examination and imaging; risk group combines these findings with PSA and grade. If a report changes after expert pathology review or surgery, that does not necessarily mean the first report was negligent. Sampling and interpretation are imperfect. Ask which difference would change surveillance, treatment intensity or additional imaging.

Supporting sources: National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab · AUA/ASTRO (2026), opens source in new tab · European Association of Urology (2026), opens source in new tab

Questions to bring to your appointment

  • What are my Grade Group, stage and risk category?
  • Which result is most influential in the recommendation?
  • Would pathology review or further imaging change the plan?
  • What is uncertain despite the tests already completed?
