MRI and prostate biopsy

How imaging and sampling work together, why MRI can miss cancer, and what biopsy route changes.

Updated November 6, 2026 · Men's health education · Not clinically peer reviewed

An MRI pictures areas of suspicion; a biopsy samples tissue to establish cancer and grade. They answer related but distinct questions, and neither is perfect in isolation.

Key distinction: PREVENT infection counts apply to the trial's specific methods and follow-up.

What PI-RADS communicates

Prostate MRI produces images radiologists use to identify suspicious regions. PI-RADS assigns scores that describe how likely a lesion is to harbor clinically significant cancer. Quality, reader experience, technical protocol and prostate characteristics affect how useful the scan is.

A low-suspicion MRI can reduce the chance of significant cancer but cannot exclude it. When other risk information remains concerning, a clinician may still recommend biopsy despite a reassuring scan.

Targeted and systematic sampling

MRI-targeted biopsy samples suspicious areas. Systematic cores sample additional regions because MRI can miss lesions or underestimate their extent. The preferred combination depends on whether the patient has had a prior biopsy, overall risk, imaging quality and guideline recommendations.

A biopsy reports Gleason patterns and Grade Group from sampled tissue. Sampling error matters, and a higher grade may occasionally be found on later biopsy or after surgery.

Supporting sources: AUA/SUO (2026), opens source in new tab · European Association of Urology (2026), opens source in new tab

Transperineal and transrectal routes

A transperineal biopsy passes the needle through disinfected skin between the scrotum and anus; a transrectal biopsy passes through the rectal wall. Both can obtain targeted and systematic cores. Practical considerations include anesthesia, discomfort, infection prevention, local expertise and access.

The final PREVENT randomized study compared transperineal biopsy without prophylactic antibiotics with transrectal biopsy using tailored prophylaxis. No infections were reported among 372 transperineal participants, compared with six among 370 transrectal participants; detection of higher-grade cancer was similar. This is an important comparison, not a guarantee of zero risk in every clinic.

Supporting sources: JAMA Oncology (2024), opens source in new tab

Emerging imaging techniques

Shorter biparametric MRI and high-resolution micro-ultrasound are being studied or adopted in selected settings. PSMA PET has established roles in staging or recurrence for certain patients but is not a default substitute for MRI or tissue biopsy at first diagnosis.

Ask which test answers the current question and whether repeating a scan would change the decision. Avoid adding imaging solely because a headline describes a novel machine or algorithm.

Supporting sources: European Association of Urology (2026), opens source in new tab · UCSF/UCLA (2020), opens source in new tab

What a negative MRI or benign sample leaves unresolved

A PI-RADS assessment reflects image suspicion, not a tissue diagnosis. A lesion's appearance can be affected by inflammation, scan quality and interpretation, and some clinically important tumors remain difficult to see. When a scan is negative, the next step depends on the pretest risk and findings such as PSA density and family history; no universal MRI result can cancel every reason for biopsy.

Biopsy cores represent only sampled tissue. Targeting an MRI lesion may improve sampling of that lesion, while systematic or perilesional cores can identify disease outside the target. Transperineal technique can alter infection exposure but also changes equipment, anesthesia and workflow. Ask the team how they address missed lesions, pathology disagreement and a plan if results remain discordant.

Supporting sources: American College of Radiology (2025), opens source in new tab · European Association of Urology (2026), opens source in new tab · JAMA Oncology (2024), opens source in new tab

Questions to bring to your appointment

  • Would a negative MRI change my need for biopsy?
  • Will the biopsy use targeted, systematic or both forms of sampling?
  • Which route is used and how is infection risk managed?
  • When and how will the pathology be explained?
