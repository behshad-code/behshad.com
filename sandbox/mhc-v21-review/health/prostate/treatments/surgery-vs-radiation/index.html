Surgery or radiation: comparing the tradeoffs

Two established local treatments, different recovery experiences, and the questions that matter before choosing.

Updated November 6, 2026 · Independent educational writing · Not clinically peer reviewed

For many people with localized prostate cancer, both surgery and radiation can provide excellent cancer control. The meaningful comparison depends on risk group, existing function, treatment logistics and the consequences of each option.

Key distinction: No trial establishes a universal winner for all ages, stages and treatment settings.

Start with who is eligible

A newly diagnosed patient needs to understand Grade Group, PSA, stage, MRI and any additional risk findings before deciding whether active surveillance, surgery or radiation should be discussed. Favorable low-risk disease often does not require immediate treatment. Higher-risk cancers may need intensified local or combined therapy.

Age and general health matter, but they do not make surgery or radiation automatically superior. A urologic oncologist and radiation oncologist can explain why different options may be appropriate for the same findings.

Supporting sources: AUA/ASTRO (2026), opens source in new tab · National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab

What surgery offers and risks

Radical prostatectomy removes the gland and seminal vesicles, provides a fuller pathology specimen, and generally leads PSA to fall to very low or undetectable levels. Recovery involves anesthesia, a catheter, activity restrictions, and possible urinary leakage or erectile changes. Some men later need salvage radiation or systemic therapy.

Robotic surgery is less invasive than open surgery in important respects, but it does not guarantee better long-term continence or erectile function. A randomized Brisbane study found similar urinary and sexual outcomes at two years, although surgical technologies and practices have evolved since that trial.

Supporting sources: Lancet Oncology (2018), opens source in new tab · National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab

What radiation offers and risks

External-beam radiation treats the prostate without removing it. Options include different daily schedules and five-session SBRT for eligible lower-risk patients. Brachytherapy places a radiation source inside or near the gland and is suitable for selected patients.

Radiation may cause urinary urgency, bowel changes, fatigue or later erectile difficulties. Hormone therapy is sometimes recommended with radiation depending on cancer risk. Because the prostate remains, PSA falls and is interpreted differently after radiation than after surgery.

Supporting sources: New England Journal of Medicine (2024), opens source in new tab · National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab

The evidence is not a universal head-to-head verdict

The 15-year ProtecT randomized results provide important context about cancer-specific outcomes and tradeoffs among assigned treatments, but that trial's active-monitoring methods differ from modern active surveillance and treatment techniques have evolved. It does not provide a personalized ranking of current surgical and radiation approaches.

Ask each specialist to estimate cancer control, urinary, bowel and sexual outcomes using patients like you, and to explain how they measure those outcomes. A second opinion may clarify an irreversible decision.

Supporting sources: New England Journal of Medicine (2023), opens source in new tab · AUA/ASTRO (2026), opens source in new tab

A practical comparison framework, not a winner-takes-all verdict

Begin by comparing the outcomes for patients with the same risk group and general health, not simply all patients receiving a particular treatment. Surgery and radiation can each be appropriate for selected localized cancers. The operation supplies definitive pathology and an early post-treatment PSA baseline but carries surgical risks and potential urinary leakage. Radiation may preserve the gland and avoid surgery while bringing bowel, urinary or sexual effects that can develop later.

The best conversation addresses cancer control, possible need for ADT, side effects at one and several years, recovery time, follow-up and what salvage options remain. The ProtecT trial provides long-term comparisons across treatment strategies in selected localized disease; PACE-B compares radiation schedules, not surgery versus radiation. A balanced decision often benefits from both urologic and radiation oncology perspectives.

Supporting sources: New England Journal of Medicine (2023), opens source in new tab · AUA/ASTRO (2026), opens source in new tab · National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab · New England Journal of Medicine (2024), opens source in new tab

Questions to bring to your appointment

  • Why is my disease risk group suitable for one or both options?
  • How are your own functional and cancer-control outcomes measured?
  • What happens to PSA after treatment, and how would recurrence be recognized?
  • If the first treatment fails, what salvage options remain?
