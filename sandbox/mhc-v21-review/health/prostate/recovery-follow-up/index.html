Life after treatment: recovery, side effects and PSA follow-up

A treatment decision should include the weeks, months and years that come after it. Patients benefit from knowing the recovery plan, whom to call, how quality-of-life changes are addressed and how follow-up results will be interpreted.

Prepare for recovery before treatment

Ask the treatment team what recovery will look like for the specific procedure or radiation plan. Pre-treatment discussions may include general fitness, medication review, pelvic floor muscle training, sexual health, caregiving arrangements, time away from work and travel plans.

Do not start, stop or adjust prescriptions, supplements, blood thinners or exercise routines based on a generic website checklist. Surgery preparation, bowel preparation and medication instructions differ across institutions and individual medical situations. Request written instructions from the treating team and reconcile any contradictory directions before the procedure.

Health during androgen deprivation therapy

ADT may affect fatigue, muscle strength, bone density, body composition, lipids and cardiovascular health. Ask about an appropriate combination of aerobic and resistance exercise, bone-health assessment and management of blood pressure, diabetes and cholesterol. Any plan needs to consider existing conditions and bone metastases when present.

If surgery is planned

After radical prostatectomy, patients may go home with a urinary catheter for a period determined by the surgeon. Ask for a demonstration of catheter bag positioning and care, a contact number for drainage problems, activity restrictions, showering instructions and the date of removal.

Short-term urinary leakage is common, especially after the catheter is removed. Pelvic floor training may help recovery and can be discussed before surgery and afterward. Urinary control may continue improving for months; persistent or bothersome leakage deserves assessment and has treatment options.

Know the team's urgent-contact instructions for fever, inability to drain urine, concerning bleeding, calf swelling, chest pain or shortness of breath. Chest pain or breathlessness requires emergency evaluation.

Understand how continence is measured

Published continence rates can differ substantially because some studies count only zero-pad recovery while others include a precautionary pad. Ask how a surgeon defines outcomes and when a pelvic-floor specialist should evaluate persistent or bothersome leakage. Continence recovery explained →

Sexual function, intimacy and emotional health

Sexual function can change after both surgery and radiation. After prostatectomy, erections may recover gradually and may be affected by age, previous function, medical conditions and whether nerve-sparing was possible. Treatment teams may discuss medicines, vacuum devices, specialized rehabilitation or other options when appropriate.

These conversations can be difficult but are legitimate medical issues, not minor side effects. Consider asking for a sexual medicine consultation and involving a partner if desired. Emotional stress, relationship concerns and anxiety before follow-up tests also deserve attention; a social worker, counselor or support group may be helpful.

Sexual recovery includes more than erections

Treatment may change desire, orgasm, ejaculation, sensation, urinary leakage with sexual activity, intimacy and relationships. Different people prioritize different sexual functions and practices. After prostatectomy, semen ejaculation ceases even when orgasm is possible. Ask about sexual-medicine care and counseling when helpful. A broader sexual health guide →

Understanding PSA after treatment

After prostatectomyAfter radiation
The prostate has been removed, so PSA is typically expected to fall to very low or undetectable levels.The prostate remains, so PSA often declines gradually and may remain measurable.
Follow-up looks at whether PSA stays low and, if detectable, whether a confirmed rising trend develops.Clinicians follow the pattern relative to the lowest value reached (nadir), with different definitions of biochemical recurrence.
Ultrasensitive assays may detect very low PSA values. At the assay's lower limits, tiny differences may reflect measurement variation.Single measurements may fluctuate; the treating team interprets results in context.

The AUA defines biochemical recurrence after prostatectomy using PSA at or above 0.2 ng/mL with confirmatory testing. In certain higher-risk situations a clinician may use ultrasensitive values earlier, but the guideline advises confirming a rising trend before taking action below that threshold. A single small change is not enough to conclude that cancer has returned.

A negative scan does not cancel an important PSA trend

PSMA PET may help locate recurrent disease, but a negative scan at a low PSA does not prove microscopic cancer is absent. Depending on prior pathology and PSA pattern, waiting for visible disease could affect the opportunity for salvage treatment. Ask when treatment should be considered even if imaging is unrevealing.

Supporting evidence: UCSF/UCLA (2020) ↗ · European Association of Urology (2026) ↗

Build a clear follow-up plan

Questions for a recovery or surveillance visit

  • When and how will the final pathology or radiation summary be explained?
  • Who will manage urinary and sexual side effects if they persist?
  • What is my exact PSA schedule, and which laboratory or assay should be used?
  • Which results need repeating, and what would prompt more evaluation?
  • Which symptoms should trigger a same-day call or emergency care?
  • Who is responsible for incidental findings or referrals that remain open?

Keep records of treatments received, relevant pathology, medications, follow-up appointments and unanswered questions. The goal is to make the next conversation easier and more precise.

Follow up the late effects as well

Urinary or rectal bleeding, bowel changes, persistent pain and changing daily function can appear months or years after radiation or other treatment. These symptoms deserve assessment; do not assume that all bleeding is benign treatment injury. A survivorship plan should name who coordinates cancer surveillance, rehabilitation and emotional support. Read the long-term health guide →
