An executive experience in post-merger integration

Integrate first.
Transform with intention.

Bringing together two established global technology businesses meant much more than combining applications. It required an integration strategy that protected customers, aligned operating models, created accountability, and prepared the enterprise for what came next.

My role

I entered the transaction as CIO of the acquired company and its global IT leader. I represented the technology workstream during planning and integration, then assumed expanded responsibilities for global business applications, architecture, analytics, PMO, BRM and engineering services.

01 / The business situation

Two global companies.
One operating future.

The combination expanded the business across broadband, satellite, video, networking and connected devices. Underneath the product portfolios were different processes, legal entities, systems, supply chains and ways of working. The challenge was to bring those operations together without making integration itself a source of business instability.

Commercial complexity

Customers could not wait

Quotes, orders, credit, delivery commitments, services and partner relationships needed to keep functioning throughout the transition.

Operational complexity

Global supply chains

Regional manufacturing, outsourced partners, logistics, sourcing, planning, quality and repair depended on different business processes.

Enterprise complexity

Systems behind every decision

ERP, PLM, CRM, HR, reporting, identity and infrastructure had to evolve together, with shared data and controls.

“The question wasn't just how to merge the systems. It was how to keep the business running while deciding what the combined company should become.”
The strategic tension

Integration speed versus enterprise risk

A fast technical consolidation could concentrate process, customer and financial risk. Excessive coexistence could perpetuate duplicated costs and operational complexity. The execution approach had to balance both.

02 / Understand before you integrate

A decision made with eyes open.

After the public announcement, management presentations and joint integration planning brought the two sides together. My role began from the acquired company: representing its global IT environment, explaining its capabilities and risks, and working with counterparts to examine what an achievable integration would require.

22 April 2015

A new global business, before integration began

EVIDENCE BASIS
Why the pre-close legal boundary mattered ↗

Joint planning could begin before closing, but the companies had to remain independent competitors until legal clearance. The kickoff materials established information-sharing restrictions and distinguished permissible planning from post-close implementation. Management teams could examine architecture and process issues without treating the businesses as already combined.

What the teams actually compared ↗

Systems of record, automation levels, process owners, manufacturing differences, supplier collaboration, order promising, invoicing, planning, inventory, product data, finance, legal entities, tax impacts and migration cost. Sharing a software vendor did not make the operating models identical.

03 / The defining choice

Transfer, not transform.

The joint teams evaluated six initial systems integration scenarios. By January 2016 the program had aligned on transferring the acquired business into the parent's existing Oracle 11i operating environment rather than attempting a full simultaneous enterprise redesign.

POTENTIAL BUSINESS ADVANTAGE

COST / RISK TO CONSIDER

Decision principle: Choose an executable integration path for the immediate business, while retaining a separate longer-term architecture roadmap.

What changed immediately

Common customer order handling, financial and tax alignment, data conversion, process adoption and a common system strategy became central to the integration program.

What deliberately waited

Broad business process redesign, a future ERP generation and functionality unrelated to the initial consolidation were not the immediate integration objective.

04 / Operate from the first day

Day 1 was a beginning.
Not a finish line.

The legal close took place on January 4, 2016. The integration team used January 5 as operational Day 1, supported by readiness checklists across IT, HR, finance, operations, sales and communications. Selected systems continued to coexist as longer migrations proceeded.

GATE 01

Readiness

Email, collaboration, employee access, networks, support paths, financial and customer continuity.

GATE 02

First 100 days

Owners, workplans, milestones, risks, training, issue management and cross-functional communication.

GATE 03

Core integration

ERP and product data conversions, finance and supply chain changes, testing and cutover planning.

GATE 04

Stabilization

Operational support, reconciliations, handoffs, remaining work and eventual retirement of legacy systems.

Continuity was designed in, not assumed.

The January 7 IMO materials marked the Day 1 readiness checklist complete. They also documented active follow-up for access provisioning, collaboration and business systems. That is normal for a staged integration: readiness to operate is not identical to full platform convergence.

By April, unresolved activities were explicitly tracked beyond the first 100 days rather than simply treated as completed.

Concrete example

One identity, multiple systems, controlled coexistence

Employees received access to the parent organization's collaboration environment while some devices, networks, applications and helpdesks remained in place during transition. Cross-company permissions were provisioned in stages with ongoing support.

Systems integration required another level of rigor

Selected approach: Initial consolidation into existing parent-company ERP and related applications.

Execution disciplines: Master data mapping, legal entity requirements, design approval, conversions, integration testing, user readiness and downtime communication.

Target evolution: An original July 2016 ERP target moved to an August 22 target in subsequent plans.

Evidence boundary: Available decks establish that target and extensive preparation; they do not independently verify the actual cutover and hypercare exit.

Explore a specific business-critical dependency ↗

Open orders, customer credit, pricing, legal-entity changes, inventory and receivables had to be reconciled before conversion. The teams considered whether to migrate or allow some orders to run down in the legacy environment. The March workplans recorded agreement on the general approach while business approvals and detailed execution continued.

How enterprise architecture became a management discipline ↗

The architecture work established a framework covering business, applications, information, technology and governance, along with an application portfolio process assessing value, health, total cost, and risk. Proposed portfolio dispositions included remove, remain, redevelop and replace. Integration work and future ERP initiatives were explicit candidates for applying the framework. Actual adoption outcomes require separate verification.

07 / Business value creation

Business value was the purpose.
Integration was the work.

The acquisition's strategic case was about scale and capability, supported by a substantial enterprise-wide synergy agenda. Technology and business systems integration were enablers of that strategy, but program delivery measures, projected savings, and corporate financial results must not be confused.

What integration work was designed to enable

The attribution standard: The acquisition established company-wide value objectives. My leadership contributed through integration planning, technology execution, a global operating organization and architecture strategy. Without finance-validated measures, this experience should not claim a specific portion of the corporate synergies as my personal savings result.

08 / What followed

Stabilize today.
Build capacity for tomorrow.

Acquisition integration establishes the combined business. A later transformation asks how that business could operate more effectively. Those are related challenges, but they are not the same program.

The leadership principles I take from this experience

01

Sequence the change

Protect immediate operations without confusing the integration platform with the final enterprise architecture.

02

Make ownership visible

Milestones, dependencies, business process decisions, training and escalation require clear cross-functional accountability.

03

Measure value, not just activity

Task completion tells you whether work is moving. Business performance and validated synergies tell you whether it created value.

The next enterprise chapter

Business 2.0

A subsequent and separate enterprise transformation initiative addressed broader process, data and operating-model complexity. Its $40–57M estimated annual run-rate benefit opportunity was a projected transformation business case, not a documented acquisition synergy result.

The larger point

My responsibility was to help turn a major corporate transaction into an operating enterprise: understand the inherited business, support its continuity, coordinate decisions, develop the people and capabilities to run it, and make a more deliberate future architecture possible.
