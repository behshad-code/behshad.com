Questions to ask a prostate surgeon

A printable guide covering surgeon experience, nerve sparing, recovery and follow-up.

Editorial draft v2.0 · Evidence checked November 6, 2026 · Not clinically peer reviewed

The most useful surgical consultation explains why surgery is recommended, what the plan is, what the realistic outcomes are, and how recovery and later PSA monitoring will be supported.

Key distinction: The questions are educational prompts, not a substitute for informed consent.

Before choosing surgery

Ask which findings establish your risk category and why surgery is preferred over monitoring or radiation. Request comparable evidence for expected cancer control and side effects. A second opinion from radiation oncology may clarify the decision.

Ask who will perform the procedure, who supervises any trainees, and how complications are handled. Case volume can reflect experience, but is not a complete quality measure.

Supporting sources: AUA/ASTRO (2026), opens source in new tab · Lancet Oncology (2018), opens source in new tab

The operative plan

Discuss the proposed access route, whether nerve sparing is reasonable on each side, the need for lymph-node dissection and circumstances that could change the operation. Ask whether intraoperative frozen-section assessment is available or indicated.

Cancer margin safety can limit nerve preservation. A realistic consent conversation addresses this uncertainty rather than guaranteeing full sexual function after surgery.

Supporting sources: Lancet Oncology (2025), opens source in new tab · PLoS ONE (2022), opens source in new tab

Recovery and function

Ask the center how it defines continence and erections in its reported rates. A zero-pad outcome and a one-safety-pad outcome are different. Baseline health and nerve preservation strongly influence sexual recovery.

Confirm catheter instructions, activity restrictions, potential complications, pelvic-floor therapy and sexual rehabilitation. Learn which symptoms require urgent contact and who coordinates follow-up.

Supporting sources: American Urological Association (2024), opens source in new tab · National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab

What happens after pathology

Know when the final pathology will be reviewed, who will order and interpret PSA tests, and what might prompt additional treatment discussions. Higher-risk pathology can change follow-up or make salvage treatment relevant.

Write down questions and request written discharge and monitoring instructions. The printable checklist can help ensure that priorities are discussed.

Supporting sources: National Cancer Institute (2026), opens source in new tab · AUA/ASTRO (2026), opens source in new tab

Questions to bring to your appointment

  • Why do you recommend surgery rather than reasonable alternatives?
  • How experienced is the team with this specific approach and risk group?
  • What is my nerve-sparing and cancer-margin strategy?
  • What are the catheter, rehabilitation and PSA follow-up plans?
